Today's issue covers five policy shocks from Nov 12, 2026: a PJM market-monitor report that puts a price on data center load, an extra €10 billion in free EU carbon allowances, draft EU sustainability assurance standards, a UK proposal for deforestation due diligence, and a threat to double US tariffs on Mexico.

01 · Energy & AI Data Centers

PJM market monitor says data center load has added $29.4B to capacity costs and faults PJM for not making data centers pay

Bloomberg.com · Nov 12 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov 12, 2026, Monitoring Analytics, PJM's independent market monitor, released its state-of-the-market report for Jan–Sep 2026. It found the energy market competitive. It also found that counting existing and forecast data center demand in the capacity market has caused an 'irreversible' $29.4 billion increase in capacity costs, and has raised total wholesale cost by $9.95/MWh (9.8%), from $101.66 to $111.61. The monitor expects the December capacity auction to clear at the maximum price again, adding another $5–10 billion. Note: these facts come from the monitor's press release and Penn Capital-Star; the Bloomberg original could not be checked.

BackgroundPJM is the largest US grid, covering the mid-Atlantic and parts of the Midwest. Its capacity market procures future resource adequacy from load forecasts, and those forecasts now include large expected data center demand. The PJM governors, the White House and a 'Ratepayer Protection Pledge' agreed that data centers should pay their own costs. FERC rejected PJM's planned 'reliability backstop auction' and much of its large-load framework. PJM has filed amended proposals, so the backstop auction cannot happen before December.
ProblemThe issue is who pays for capacity procured to serve data centers. Today the cost is spread across all ratepayers through capacity prices. The monitor recommends leaving forecast data center demand out of the capacity market and requiring data centers to bring their own new generation. PJM says it cannot control how costs are allocated, that this is up to state regulators, and that its proposals give states tools to assign costs to large-load customers.
Where & whyThe problem sits where load forecasting meets capacity procurement in the wholesale market. Speculative large-load forecasts raise procurement and push prices toward the cap, and the cost is socialized through uniform clearing prices. Three frictions drive it. First, jurisdiction is split: FERC and PJM run wholesale design, while states set retail cost allocation. Second, the information about whether data center projects will actually materialize is asymmetric. Third, data centers that do not bear the procurement cost have weak incentives to self-supply.
Stakeholders
Residential and small commercial ratepayers — Face billions in higher capacity charges driven by load they did not create
Data center developers and hyperscalers — Want fast interconnection without bearing the full cost of capacity or new generation
PJM Interconnection — Must keep the grid reliable and win FERC approval for its large-load framework, while denying it has authority over cost allocation
State utility regulators and governors — Under political pressure to protect ratepayers; may need large-load tariffs to assign costs
Generators — Gain from capacity prices clearing at the cap
Research hints
💡 Treat forecast data center load as a demand signal with misaligned incentives: what happens to procured capacity, and who bears the risk, when forecast load does not show up?
💡 Compare cost allocation through uniform capacity prices with 'bring your own generation' and large-load tariff designs, for example through a mechanism-design or cost-causation lens.
💡 The PJM–FERC–state split creates jurisdictional variation; states that adopt large-load tariffs at different times could serve as comparison groups.
Data leadsPJM capacity auction results and load forecast reports (Data Miner), Monitoring Analytics state-of-the-market reports, FERC eLibrary dockets on PJM's large-load and backstop auction filings, and state PUC large-load tariff dockets.
Keywordscapacity markets · data center electricity demand · large-load tariffs · load forecasting · federal-state jurisdiction
Related research
Peak-Load Energy Management by Direct Load Control Contracts (Management Science, 2022)
Models utility direct load control contracts for curtailing peak demand, a starting point for designing flexibility obligations for large loads like data centers instead of procuring firm capacity for them.
02 · Trade & Industrial Policy

EU deal adds 121 million free ETS allowances (about €10B) for energy-intensive industry in 2026–2030

ESG Today · Nov 13 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov 12, 2026, the European Parliament and Council reached a provisional deal to add 121 million free EU ETS allowances for 2026–2030, worth about €10 billion depending on carbon prices. Of these, 5 million are reserved for sectors highly exposed to carbon leakage. The deal goes beyond the Commission's July proposal of 88 million extra allowances for sectors under the heat and fuel benchmarks.

BackgroundFree allocation shields industries at risk of carbon leakage, meaning production moving to countries with weaker climate rules. The heat and fuel benchmarks make up about 25% of free allocation and cover chemicals, pulp and paper, food and beverage, ceramics and glass. Member states pushed for relief because of high energy prices, which ESG Today links to the Russia-Ukraine and Iran wars. The ETS has raised more than €270 billion since 2005, and the Commission credits it with a 50% emissions cut in covered sectors.
ProblemExtra free allowances lower firms' average carbon costs and may ease leakage and competitiveness pressure. They can also weaken the incentive to invest in abatement and cut auction revenue. The trade-off is between keeping industrial production in Europe and keeping a strong carbon price signal. Parliament and Council still have to endorse the deal.
Where & whyThe problem arises in the allocation rules, specifically the benchmark-based free allocation to heat- and fuel-intensive processes. It reflects a tension between the marginal and the average carbon price under energy-price stress. Free allocation tied to production keeps output in place, but the opportunity cost of an allowance should, in theory, still drive abatement. Whether firms actually behave this way depends on liquidity constraints and on how allocation updates with output.
Stakeholders
Energy-intensive manufacturers (chemicals, paper, food, ceramics, glass) — Lower compliance costs and less leakage pressure
Member states — Industrial jobs versus lower ETS auction revenue
European Commission — Keeping the ETS credible while pursuing a 'competitiveness and independence' agenda
Low-carbon technology providers and abatement investors — A weaker price signal may delay demand for decarbonization
Research hints
💡 Use free allocation as a policy shock: compare installations in heat/fuel-benchmark sectors with those under product benchmarks, before and after the deal.
💡 Look at the operational compliance levers: does more free allocation shift firms from at-source abatement toward holding allowances?
💡 Puzzle: if the opportunity cost of an allowance is unchanged, why would free allocation change production location at all? Consider liquidity, output-based updating and managerial attention.
Data leadsEU Transaction Log (EUTL) installation-level verified emissions and free allocation, matched to firm financials (Compustat Global or Orbis) and trade flows by product from UN Comtrade.
Keywordsindustrial policy · cap and trade · carbon leakage · free allocation · cost pass-through
Related research
Operational Compliance Levers, Environmental Performance, and Firm Performance Under Cap and Trade Regulation (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2012)
Empirically links operational compliance levers (prevention, end-of-pipe control, allowance use) under cap and trade to environmental and firm performance, a direct frame for how extra free allowances change behavior.
Compliance Strategies under Permits for Emissions (Production and Operations Management, 2007)
Models firms' choice between abatement investment and permit acquisition, which helps predict how a larger free allocation shifts that choice.
03 · ESG & Disclosure

EU Commission consults on draft CSRD limited-assurance standard based on ISSA 5000; comments due Jan 18, 2027

ESG Today · Nov 12 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov 12, 2026, the European Commission opened a consultation on a draft limited assurance standard for CSRD sustainability reports. The Committee of European Auditing Oversight Bodies (CEAOB) drafted it from the IAASB's ISSA 5000. It drops the reasonable-assurance provisions and adds requirements on EU Taxonomy eligibility and alignment, digital reporting, and use of third-party verification reports. The mandatory start date (2027, 2028 or later) is open for comment until Jan 18, 2027.

BackgroundCSRD reporters must obtain independent external assurance. The Omnibus I simplification package removed the planned move to stricter 'reasonable assurance', so limited assurance becomes the permanent level. Until now, practice has relied on a patchwork of national and international standards.
ProblemThe standard will decide how much of the reported sustainability data, including value-chain and Taxonomy data, is actually checked, and at what cost. Limited assurance is cheaper but gives less confidence, and the start date affects both compliance costs and how comparable data are across firms.
Where & whyThe problem sits at the verification stage between preparers, assurance providers and users. Sustainability data, especially supply chain data gathered from upstream partners, has high information asymmetry. Under limited assurance, auditors rely more on management's and third parties' representations, including reliance on third-party verification reports. Data quality therefore depends on the incentives of suppliers and certifiers who are outside the audit's direct scope.
Stakeholders
CSRD-reporting companies — Want lower assurance costs and a clear, later start date
Audit firms and other assurance providers — Scope of engagements, methodology and liability
Investors and data users — Want reliable, comparable ESG and Taxonomy data
Suppliers in value chains — May face more data requests and verification as reporters seek evidence they can assure
Research hints
💡 A staggered or delayed mandatory date would create variation in assurance requirements across firms and years.
💡 Think of assurance level as an information-design choice: when does limited assurance plus third-party certifications substitute for, or complement, deeper verification?
💡 Compare assured and unassured value-chain metrics (for example scope 3) for later restatements or for divergence from ratings.
Data leadsAssurance flags in LSEG ESG (whether the CSR report is externally audited) and Audit Analytics, combined with hand-collected CSRD sustainability statements and assurance reports from company annual reports.
Keywordsmandatory climate reporting · sustainability assurance · scope 3 disclosure · sustainable finance taxonomy · sustainability standards
Related research
Sustainable evaluation and verification in supply chains: Aligning and leveraging accountability to stakeholders (Journal of Operations Management, 2015)
Examines how firms evaluate and verify sustainability across supply chains to meet stakeholder accountability, which is central to what limited assurance can actually vouch for.
Sustainability in multi‐tier supply chains: Understanding the double agency role of the first‐tier supplier (Journal of Operations Management, 2015)
Shows first-tier suppliers acting as agents for sustainability compliance further upstream, the channel through which assured value-chain data is often produced.
04 · Food & Agriculture

UK consults on mandatory deforestation due diligence for seven forest-risk commodities, targeting illegal deforestation only

ESG Today · Nov 12 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov 12, 2026, the UK opened a consultation, open until Jan 17, 2027, on the long-delayed Forest Risk Commodities regulations. Businesses with global revenue above £1 million that use coffee, cocoa, cattle, soy, oil palm, rubber or timber would have to trace origin with geolocation data and reduce risk to a negligible level. Products would be identified by HS codes, and the consultation covers annual reporting and fines.

BackgroundThe rules were delayed about five years. They cover the same commodity categories as the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR). The UK government links UK consumption to about 29,000 hectares of deforestation and 9.4 million tonnes of related emissions a year.
ProblemUnlike the EUDR, the UK rules would target only illegal deforestation, although the government says it wants to move to a deforestation-free standard later. Firms supplying both markets would face two overlapping but different compliance standards. A narrower legality test may also allow legally cleared land to keep supplying the UK. Final rules, start dates and penalties are not yet set.
Where & whyThe problem arises at the upstream sourcing and traceability stage. Commodities are aggregated from many smallholders, which makes plot-level geolocation costly. Legality depends on producer-country law, so the risk assessment is only as good as local enforcement. The £1 million threshold and the HS-code scope set where compliance burdens fall and where firms may reroute trade or switch suppliers.
Stakeholders
UK importers, processors and retailers — Traceability and due diligence costs, and possible supplier switching
Smallholder producers in exporting countries — Risk of exclusion if they cannot provide geolocation data
UK and EU regulators — Credibility of enforcement, and how far the UK and EU regimes align
Environmental NGOs — Want a deforestation-free standard rather than a legality-only test
Research hints
💡 Two regimes with different stringency (UK legality-only versus EU deforestation-free) set up a comparison of sourcing and trade diversion between the two markets.
💡 Use a supplier-selection or traceability-investment model: when do buyers drop hard-to-trace smallholders instead of investing in traceability?
💡 Watch the £1 million revenue threshold and HS-code scope for bunching or for product reclassification.
Data leadsUN Comtrade and HMRC trade data by HS code and origin, Trase commodity supply chain data linking exporters to sourcing regions, and Global Forest Watch tree cover loss.
Keywordsdeforestation · mandatory due diligence · multi-tier traceability · smallholder inclusion · trade diversion
Related research
Toward “Omniscient” supply chains: Enabling and incentivizing sustainable operations (Production and Operations Management, 2026)
Analyzes how to enable and incentivize full value-chain sustainability due diligence when market and regulatory tools fall short, which speaks directly to geolocation-based traceability mandates.
Blockchain for Supply Chain Traceability: Business Requirements and Critical Success Factors (Production and Operations Management, 2019)
Sets out business requirements and success factors for supply chain traceability systems, relevant to the cost of meeting origin-tracing rules.
05 · Supply Chain Resilience

Trump threatens to immediately double tariffs on Mexico over migrant caravans; scope and legal basis unclear

Supply Chain Dive · Nov 12 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov 12, 2026, Trump posted on Truth Social that he would immediately double tariffs on Mexico unless it stops migrant caravans heading to the US. He tied the caravans to Republicans' 'perceived loss' in the previous week's midterms. He did not say which tariffs or how the increase would work. Mexico News Daily suggests the tariffs on Mexican steel, aluminum and vehicles could be the ones doubled, but the White House has not confirmed this.

BackgroundMexico already faces a 10% tariff following a forced-labor investigation launched in March, and is part of a March US investigation into manufacturing capacity policies. The USMCA review is under way. With a Democrat-led Congress, three bills (S. 5390, S. 1272 and H.R. 7557) could limit the president's tariff powers. President Sheinbaum said no caravan has reached the border this term. She acknowledged two recent caravans, one of which shrank from 500–600 people to 100 or fewer.
ProblemFirms with nearshored North American supply chains face sudden uncertainty about which goods, rates and start date a tariff increase would cover, and on what legal basis. They have to decide whether to pre-buy, build inventory, reroute or reshore, or simply wait, without knowing whether the threat will be carried out.
Where & whyThe problem arises at the cross-border sourcing link between Mexico and the US, especially in integrated industries such as autos and metals. Tariff policy is being used as leverage on a non-trade issue (migration), so its timing and scope are hard to predict. Congress may constrain the president's tariff powers and the USMCA review adds another layer of uncertainty. Both make the expected duration of any shock unclear, which is what drives the trade-off between hedging and waiting.
Stakeholders
US manufacturers and automakers sourcing from Mexico — Higher input costs and disrupted integrated cross-border production
Mexican government — Export market access and leverage in the USMCA review
US Congress (Democrat-led) — Whether to curb the president's tariff authority through pending bills
Cross-border logistics providers — Volatile volumes from pre-buying and sudden pauses in shipments
Research hints
💡 Separate a tariff threat from an implemented tariff: do threats alone trigger pre-buying or supplier diversification, as seen in import spikes?
💡 Think of supply chain choice as a real option under policy uncertainty: when does a firm diversify versus concentrate its sourcing?
💡 Event study: compare equity reactions of firms with heavy Mexico exposure with those of firms with little.
Data leadsUSITC DataWeb and Census monthly imports from Mexico by HTS line (steel, aluminum, vehicles), BTS TransBorder freight flows, Federal Register tariff actions, and bill-of-lading data (Panjiva) for firm-level sourcing shifts.
Keywordsregulatory uncertainty · pre-buy behavior · nearshoring logistics · supplier diversification · reshoring
Related research
Diversify or Concentrate? Supply Chain Responses to Policy Uncertainty (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2025)
Tests empirically whether firms diversify or concentrate sourcing in response to domestic and upstream policy uncertainty, the core response question this threat raises.
Shock and Awe: A Theoretical Framework and Data Sources for Studying the Impact of 2025 Tariffs on Global Supply Chains (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2025)
Offers a theoretical framework and data sources for studying the supply chain effects of the 2025 tariffs and the uncertainty around them.
Thread to pull

The PJM data center fight and the EU free-allowance deal are both about who bears a cost that is socialized through a market price: capacity charges spread across ratepayers, and a carbon price softened for industry. In both, the response relies on allocation rules (large-load tariffs, free allocation) rather than changing the price itself. Similarly, the UK deforestation rules and the EU assurance standard both rely on third-party evidence from upstream, so it is worth asking how verification, legality tests and allocation rules each shift costs, and incentives, along a chain.
