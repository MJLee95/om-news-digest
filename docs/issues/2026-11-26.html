01 · Trade & Industrial Policy

USTR proposes Section 301 tariffs of 15–35% on 14 economies over 'structural excess capacity' in manufacturing

Supply Chain Dive · Nov 25 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov 25, 2026, the US Trade Representative said 14 of 16 economies investigated since March 11, 2026 have policies that create 'structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing.' It proposed additional tariffs of 15% to 35% by country. Examples: EU 15% (fallback up to 20%), Japan and Switzerland up to 15% (fallback 20%), South Korea and Taiwan up to 15% (fallback 25%), Vietnam up to 35% (20% with a trade deal) and India up to 25% (17% with a deal). Comments are due Jan 5, 2027, hearings start Jan 25, and no tariff can take effect before 2027.

BackgroundAccording to Lexology, the Section 301 actions are part of an effort to replace the country-level tariffs imposed under IEEPA, which the Supreme Court struck down. Earlier Section 301 findings already led to tariffs on 60 trade partners for allegedly failing to impose forced-labor measures. Most of the new rates would be counted after subtracting duties importers already pay, and importers would usually pay the higher of the new rate or the existing forced-labor duty, not both. Some goods are exempt. China is among the 14, but its proposed rate was not confirmed in the research notes.
ProblemImporters face a rate schedule that is conditional and still unsettled. Each country has a base rate and a fallback that depends on how negotiations go. Some countries get discounts for signing 'Agreements on Reciprocal Trade.' Rates interact with existing duties, and the start date is open. Firms must decide now whether to re-source, pre-buy, hold inventory or wait, without knowing the final rates.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the sourcing and landed-cost stage of global supply chains. It is driven by policy uncertainty: rates depend on bilateral bargaining that firms cannot observe. Netting against existing duties means the actual cost increase differs by product and origin. The comment and hearing process creates a window in which firms can lobby for exemptions or front-load imports.
Stakeholders
US importers and manufacturers — Higher landed costs and a sourcing decision under uncertain final rates and timing
Exporting economies (EU, Japan, Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, India, China and others) — Market access; whether to accept trade deals that lower their rate
USTR / US administration — Rebuilding tariff leverage on a legal basis that holds up after the IEEPA ruling
Suppliers in countries not on the list — Possible gains from trade diversion
Research hints
💡 The country-specific fallback and deal-contingent rates create variation in tariff exposure across origins at the same moment; that is the kind of cross-sectional spread a difference-in-differences design or a real-options model of sourcing could exploit.
💡 Watch for pre-buy behavior between the comment deadline and implementation, and compare it with the 2018–19 Section 301 rounds on China.
💡 Netting against existing duties means headline rates overstate the cost increase for some product lines; mapping effective incremental tariffs by HTS line is itself a contribution.
Data leadsUSITC DataWeb and Census USA Trade Online for monthly imports by HTS line and origin; Federal Register / USTR docket comments for exemption requests; Panjiva bill-of-lading data (commercial) for firm-level supplier switching.
Keywordsindustrial policy · regulatory uncertainty · supplier diversification · trade diversion · pre-buy behavior
Related research
Diversify or Concentrate? Supply Chain Responses to Policy Uncertainty (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2025)
Tests empirically whether firms diversify or concentrate sourcing when domestic and upstream policy uncertainty rises, the core decision importers face under this proposal.
Not Coming Home: Trade and Economic Policy Uncertainty in American Supply Chain Networks (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2020)
Studies how trade and economic policy uncertainty reshaped American supply chain networks, finding limited reshoring, which is a useful baseline for predicting responses here.
02 · Energy & AI Data Centers

EIA proposes mandatory annual Data Center Energy Consumption Survey (Form EIA-872) for facilities of 1 MW and above

CASRAI · Nov 26 · Read · ☆ Star

In Federal Register notice 2026-24077 (signed Nov 23, 2026; due for publication Nov 27), EIA sought comment on Form EIA-872. The survey would collect energy use and physical and operating details from individual US data centers with at least 1 MW of IT design capacity. EIA estimates about 1,400 respondents a year and 12,200 total burden hours. The survey would be fielded in 2027, with CASRAI saying 2026 is the reference year.

BackgroundUntil now, data centers were covered only within EIA's commercial buildings survey, which last collected data for 2018. In March 2026, EIA ran voluntary pilot surveys in Texas, Washington state and the Northern Virginia–DC area, covering 196 companies. Senators Warren and Hawley later said EIA had committed to a mandatory survey. Coverage spans enterprise, colocation, hyperscale, edge and crypto-mining sites, and campuses sharing power or cooling count as one reporting unit. CASRAI describes reporting as mandatory and annual; the research notes did not verify the legal basis against the notice itself.
ProblemPlanners, utilities and regulators are making grid and capacity decisions about data center load without facility-level measurements. Estimates of AI-driven demand vary widely, and the industry has disclosed data only voluntarily and unevenly. The survey's design choices will shape how useful the data are and what firms might do in response: the 1 MW threshold, one unit per campus, and publication of totals only.
Where & whyThe gap is an information asymmetry at the interface between large loads and the power system. Operators know their consumption, while the utilities, ISOs and policymakers forecasting load and approving interconnections do not. Earlier voluntary disclosure was incomplete. A mandatory census could correct this, but publishing only aggregates limits facility-level research, and the size threshold creates an incentive at the margin.
Stakeholders
Data center operators (hyperscale, colocation, crypto miners) — Reporting burden and protection of commercially sensitive operating data
EIA / DOE — Credible national statistics on data center energy use
Utilities, ISOs and state regulators — Better inputs for load forecasting, capacity planning and large-load tariffs
Policymakers (e.g., Senators Warren and Hawley) — Transparency about AI's grid and cost impacts
Research hints
💡 A reporting cutoff at 1 MW of IT capacity invites a bunching analysis: watch for facilities reported or designed just under the line.
💡 Compare the voluntary pilot regions (Texas, Washington, Northern Virginia–DC) with later mandatory results to gauge how selective voluntary disclosure was.
💡 Ask how aggregate-only publication constrains what researchers can identify, and what pairing with utility or ISO load data might recover.
Data leadsForm EIA-872 aggregates once released; EIA-861 utility sales and ERCOT large-load reports for cross-checks; the Federal Register notice 2026-24077 docket comments.
Keywordsdata center electricity demand · load forecasting · regulatory thresholds · voluntary disclosure
Related research
Bunching with the Stars: How Firms Respond to Environmental Certification (Management Science, 2022)
Shows how firms bunch strategically around ENERGY STAR certification thresholds, a lens for how operators might respond to EIA-872's 1 MW reporting cutoff.
How Sustainable Is Big Data? (Production and Operations Management, 2018)
An early OM essay on the environmental footprint of big data and computing, framing why measuring data center energy matters for sustainable operations.
03 · Supply Chain Resilience

Flooding in Thailand's eastern industrial belt cuts October vehicle output 35% to a six-year low

Thailand Business News · Nov 25 · Read · ☆ Star

Late-September floods cut off parts supplies in Thailand's eastern industrial belt. October vehicle output fell 35% year on year to 87,938, the lowest month since July 2020, and passenger-car output fell 53%. Toyota, Honda and Mitsubishi Motors ran short of parts, with Toyota Motor Thailand stopping for six days and some plants shut into mid-October. Preliminary Ministry of Industry data put business flood damage at about 18.21 billion baht, roughly 98% of it in Chonburi province.

BackgroundThailand is a major auto and auto-parts hub with heavy Japanese automaker presence. Disruption had already begun in September, when output fell 6.08% to 120,319. October output fell 38% for the domestic market (31,424) and 34% for export (56,514). Gasoline and diesel model output fell 64.23%, while battery-electric output rose 50.15%. Domestic sales actually rose 8.71% to 51,127, with battery-electric cars at 32.25% of sales. January–October output was 1,167,968, and the Federation of Thai Industries is keeping its 1.45 million target for 2026.
ProblemA regional disaster shut down parts supply concentrated in one province and halted assembly across several automakers at once. Rising sales alongside collapsing output points to a gap between supply and demand that inventories and rerouting did not cover. By the notes' own arithmetic, about 282,000 vehicles in November–December would be needed to hit the 1.45 million target.
Where & whyThe failure is in upstream parts supply clustered in Chonburi. It is a common-mode risk: assemblers that share a regional supplier base were hit together. Lean inventories and the geographic agglomeration of suppliers make the cluster efficient in normal times but fragile to a correlated hazard. The split between powertrains (internal combustion down, EV up) suggests that dependence on flooded suppliers differed by product line.
Stakeholders
Japanese automakers in Thailand (Toyota, Honda, Mitsubishi Motors) — Lost production and export commitments; decisions on buffer stocks and dual sourcing
Tier-1/2 parts suppliers in Chonburi — Physical damage and loss of customers to alternative sources
Federation of Thai Industries / Thai government — The country's credibility as an auto hub and the 1.45 million target
Export customers — Delayed deliveries
Research hints
💡 Compare with the 2011 Thai floods and the 2011 Tohoku earthquake: did automakers that built buffers or dual sourcing after 2011 recover faster this time?
💡 The divergence between internal-combustion and battery-electric output during the same shock is a puzzle about how supply networks overlap across product lines.
💡 Recovery timing (a six-day stop versus mid-October shutdowns) can be used to estimate firm-level recovery curves.
Data leadsFTI monthly production and export statistics by vehicle type; Thai Ministry of Industry flood damage data by province; UN Comtrade for Thai auto-part exports; Japanese automakers' monthly overseas production releases.
Keywordsclimate adaptation · common-mode failure · supplier region risk · inventory management
Related research
Patterns in firms’ inventories and flexibility levels after a low‐probability, high‐impact disruption event: Empirical evidence from the Great East Japan Earthquake (Production and Operations Management, 2023)
Documents how firms raised raw-material inventories and flexibility after the 2011 Japan earthquake, a direct template for studying automaker responses after these floods.
Disruption Risk and Optimal Sourcing in Multitier Supply Networks (Management Science, 2016)
Models optimal multitier sourcing when tier-2 suppliers share exposure to floods and earthquakes, the overlap that drove this disruption.
04 · ESG & Disclosure

Earthsight probe: Cargill publicly backs EU Deforestation Regulation while lobbying to weaken and delay it

news - Mongabay · Nov 25 · Read · ☆ Star

A Nov 10 report by the UK NGO Earthsight, covered by Mongabay on Nov 25, alleges that Cargill presents itself as EUDR-ready while lobbying to weaken and delay the law. The report says this works through trade associations (AmCham EU, FEDIOL, COCERAL, the German Association of Oilseed Processors), meetings with Commission officials, and meetings with critical MEPs. Cargill says it is 'actively preparing to meet EUDR requirements' and rejects the claim that it sought to weaken the law. The EUDR takes effect for large and medium-sized companies on Dec 30.

BackgroundThe EUDR requires firms to prove that soy, beef, palm oil, cocoa and other covered commodities do not come from land deforested after 2020. Cargill trades all four of those commodities. The law was delayed 12 months in late 2024, and another 12-month delay was proposed in 2025. In July 2026, cattle hides, leather, soybeans for sowing and retreaded tires were removed from its scope; the Commission said leather accounts for only 2.5–5% of deforestation in the cattle supply chain. COCERAL pushed for a product- and country-level legal database, and the Commission later proposed something similar.
ProblemThere is a gap between what the firm says publicly in support of sustainable sourcing rules and what it does politically through intermediaries. Regulation that is shaped during implementation can be diluted in ways that public disclosures do not reveal. Whether the lobbying actually caused the scope changes and delays is Earthsight's argument, not an established fact.
Where & whyThe issue arises at the rule-design and implementation stage of mandatory due diligence. Large traders hold the traceability capabilities and the compliance costs, and so have incentives to shape rules. Trade associations let firms pursue those interests without attribution, which creates an information asymmetry between public ESG positioning and actual political activity.
Stakeholders
Cargill and other commodity traders — Compliance costs, traceability investment and reputation
Trade associations (COCERAL, FEDIOL, AmCham EU, FEFAC) — Practical, workable implementation rules for members
European Commission and MEPs — A law that is both enforceable and politically viable
NGOs (Earthsight) and forest communities — Effective deforestation-free supply chains
Research hints
💡 Use corporate political activity and coalition lobbying as a lens: why route opposition through associations rather than lobbying directly?
💡 The sequence of EUDR delays and scope removals gives a series of policy events against which traders' traceability investments and sourcing could be tracked.
💡 Measuring consistency between what firms say publicly and how they lobby is an open measurement problem for ESG ratings.
Data leadsEU Transparency Register and Commission meeting records (via LobbyFacts); Trase commodity supply chain data for traders' sourcing exposure; RepRisk for incident coverage.
Keywordscorporate lobbying · deforestation · mandatory due diligence · greenwashing
Related research
A theoretical model on how firms can leverage political resources to align with supply chain strategy for competitive advantage (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2022)
Theorizes how firms use political resources to make the regulatory environment fit their supply chain strategy, which is what Earthsight alleges here.
Influence Activities, Coalitions, and Uniform Policies (Management Science, 2017)
Models influence activities and coalitions in shaping uniform policies, relevant to lobbying through trade associations.
05 · Critical Minerals & Batteries

Suzuki to develop LFP batteries with Japan's Eliiy Power amid worries over reliance on China

Nikkei Asia · Nov 26 · Read · ☆ Star

Nikkei Asia reported on Nov 26, 2026 that Suzuki will make batteries free of rare metals because it struggles to secure affordable batteries for its kei minicars and lacks Japanese sources that can power its EVs affordably. A photo caption says it will develop lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries with Japanese battery maker Eliiy Power. Investment, timeline, capacity and models were not available because the article is paywalled.

BackgroundSuzuki's first electric kei car, the e SKY, already uses a 26 kWh LFP battery from BYD's battery unit, with a claimed range of about 310 km and a starting price around $13,500. The real change therefore appears to be a shift in supplier geography, away from a Chinese supplier, rather than in chemistry. In 2025, Chinese rare-earth export limits forced Suzuki to pause Swift production. That episode concerned motor magnets, not batteries, but it shows the firm's exposure to China.
ProblemLow-cost EV segments such as kei cars depend on cheap Chinese cells. Domestic alternatives are scarcer and more expensive. Suzuki must trade off cost against supply security, and possibly scale against control, by co-developing with a smaller Japanese partner. It is unconfirmed whether this replaces BYD.
Where & whyThe friction is at the cell-sourcing stage, where China dominates. Japanese suppliers do not offer affordable LFP at the volumes Suzuki needs, a capacity and cost gap. The price-sensitive kei segment leaves little margin to absorb a supply-security premium, and past export controls make the concentration risk concrete.
Stakeholders
Suzuki — Affordable, secure battery supply for low-margin kei EVs
Eliiy Power — Scale-up opportunity as a domestic LFP partner
BYD battery unit — Possible loss of a Japanese automaker customer
Japanese government / industrial policy — Domestic battery supply chain resilience
Research hints
💡 Frame this as supplier switching under concentration risk, where chemistry stays the same but origin changes; this separates geographic diversification from technology substitution.
💡 Learning-curve models could ask how large the cost penalty of a small domestic LFP supplier is relative to Chinese scale.
Data leadsUSGS Mineral Commodity Summaries for mineral concentration; Benchmark/BNEF battery cell price and capacity data (commercial); Japanese METI battery strategy documents; Suzuki investor presentations.
Keywordssupplier concentration · supplier switching · raw material security · learning curves
Thread to pull

The Thai floods, Suzuki's move away from Chinese battery suppliers, and the country-differentiated Section 301 tariffs all concern geographic concentration risk. One source is natural, one geopolitical and one tariff-driven, and Japanese automakers sit exposed to all three. Separately, both the Section 301 exemptions and the EUDR scope cuts show policies being reshaped by firm input during a comment or revision window. Treating policy content itself as endogenous to lobbying is worth exploring before using these shocks as clean natural experiments.

Tax-induced Corporate In-kind Donations: Equilibrium Analysis and Policy Design
M&SOM

The paper models how tax incentives for in-kind food donations change a capacity-constrained firm's production and donation decisions, profits and consumer surplus, and how such incentives should be designed. Incentives can alter operational choices, not just how much is donated.

Like the Section 301 tariff schedule and the EUDR revisions, it treats policy design as something that shifts firms' operating decisions, which is a useful analytical template for evaluating policy levers.
