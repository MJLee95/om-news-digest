01 · Energy & AI Data Centers

National Grid buys 73 acres in Teesside for a substation of up to 3.2GW to serve a planned AI data center

DatacenterDynamics · Nov 02 · Read · ☆ Star

National Grid Electricity Transmission finalized the purchase of about 29.5 hectares (73 acres) at Dorman Point, Teesside, in a land agreement reached in late October 2026. The site is for a new substation expected to provide up to 3.2GW. It would feed a private-wire network serving a nearby AI data center on a former steel site and a battery energy storage system. Balfour Beatty and Steel River Power are linked to delivering the power works. The price and construction timeline were not disclosed.

BackgroundIn 2025 the UK government identified Teesworks as a prime candidate for a large AI data center complex, and the data center plan was approved in August despite objections. Teesworks is listed as the primary developer. Major US developers, Google among them, have reportedly shown interest, but no operating or investment partner has been finalized. The substation is part of National Grid's Great Grid Upgrade. Local coverage calls it potentially the UK's largest substation, though that is a projection. One locally quoted figure is 1.44GW of contracted capacity with expansion to 3.2GW, but who said it is unverified.
ProblemThe transmission owner is committing land and grid capacity at multi-gigawatt scale before the anchor data center tenant is known. That raises the question of who bears the risk if the load arrives late, arrives smaller than planned, or never arrives. The private-wire design paired with battery storage also blurs the line between shared public network assets and dedicated infrastructure for one customer.
Where & whyThe problem sits at the grid connection and transmission investment stage, which is the slowest link in AI data center siting. Grid builds have long lead times while data center demand is uncertain and lumpy, so capacity gets built speculatively. There is also an information gap: the grid owner cannot observe how firm the eventual tenant's demand is. Land ownership is itself disputed in the coverage: Teesworks versus the publicly owned South Tees Development Corporation.
Stakeholders
National Grid Electricity Transmission — Earns a regulated return on new assets but risks stranded capacity if the data center underuses the connection.
Teesworks / South Tees Development Corporation — Wants to monetize a former steel site and attract a hyperscale tenant; power availability is its main selling point.
Prospective data center developers (e.g., Google, reportedly interested) — Want fast, large, firm power; the substation lowers their time-to-power risk.
Balfour Beatty and Steel River Power — Construction and private-wire delivery contracts.
Local residents and objectors — Land use, environmental impacts and whether regeneration benefits materialize.
Research hints
💡 Real-options framing: anticipatory grid capacity built for an uncommitted anchor load, with staged expansion (contracted capacity now, more later) as a built-in option.
💡 Compare UK 'AI growth zone' anticipatory build with US large-load tariffs that require collateral or minimum bills from data centers. Who absorbs demand uncertainty under each regime?
💡 Private-wire plus co-located battery storage is a hybrid between regulated network assets and behind-the-meter supply; think about how that changes the contracting and cost-allocation problem.
Data leadsNESO Transmission Entry Capacity (TEC) register and connections queue data for UK grid connections; for US comparisons, LBNL 'Queued Up' and state PUC large-load tariff dockets.

02 · Critical Minerals & Batteries

DOE unveils national battery strategy targeting an integrated US supply chain by 2030 and launches PNNL-led Battery Manufacturing Accelerator

Charged EVs · Nov 01 · Read · ☆ Star

On October 28, 2026, DOE's Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation released a national battery strategy. Its goals are a fully integrated domestic battery supply chain by 2030 and US leadership in battery technology and manufacturing by 2035. DOE projects US battery demand across transportation and storage will nearly quadruple, from 179 GWh in 2025 to 707 GWh in 2035. DOE also launched BattMan, a manufacturing accelerator led by PNNL with Argonne, Oak Ridge and the National Laboratory of the Rockies plus five universities. No funding amount was disclosed.

BackgroundThe strategy has six pillars: upstream and midstream supply chain investment, faster lab-to-market commercialization, manufacturing process innovation, grid modernization deployment, international coordination, and workforce. One process problem BattMan targets is electrode production scrap, which Oak Ridge says can reach 40%; part of that work will happen at ORNL's Battery Manufacturing Facility in Tennessee. DOE says the accelerator will apply 'Super Intelligence' (its own term) to production processes. Context: the launch came six days before the midterms, and the EV tax credit expired in 2025.
ProblemThe federal government is signaling an onshoring push for batteries while one major demand-side support, the EV tax credit, has lapsed. The accelerator also has no disclosed budget, and its industry partners are still being recruited. Firms deciding where to site cathode, cell or recycling capacity face mixed signals about policy durability and about how quickly yield improvements will close the cost gap with incumbent overseas producers.
Where & whyThe friction is in midstream manufacturing: electrode coating and cell production, where high scrap rates and process know-how are concentrated abroad. It is also in policy uncertainty, since capacity decisions span a decade while strategies can shift with elections. The demand projection mixes EVs, whose subsidy has ended, with grid storage, which is growing, so the demand forecast that capacity is built against is itself contested.
Stakeholders
DOE / CMEI and national labs (PNNL, ORNL, Argonne, NLR) — Want to show progress on onshoring and process innovation, and need industry partners to make BattMan relevant.
US battery and materials manufacturers — Could gain yield improvements and support, but face uncertain demand and policy durability when committing capex.
Grid-storage developers and utilities — Would benefit from secure domestic cell supply for a fast-growing storage market.
Foreign incumbent battery suppliers — Risk losing US market share if domestic supply and sourcing preferences scale up.
Research hints
💡 Learning-curve and yield models: what does cutting electrode scrap from levels as high as 40% imply for the cost gap between domestic and imported cells?
💡 Policy-durability lens: compare firms' battery capacity announcements and cancellations around the 2025 EV credit expiry and this strategy launch.
💡 The strategy pairs a demand projection with supply targets; examine whether storage demand can substitute for weakened EV demand as the anchor for midstream capacity.
Data leadsSEC EDGAR 10-K/8-K capex and plant announcements, Census USA Trade Online imports of lithium-ion cells (HS 8507.60), USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries; Benchmark/BNEF if available.
Keywordsindustrial policy · reshoring · capacity investment under uncertainty · battery storage · raw material security

03 · Circular Economy

EU Joint Research Centre proposes first EU-wide end-of-waste criteria for textile reuse and remanufacturing

Ecotextile News · Nov 02 · Read · ☆ Star

The European Commission's Joint Research Centre published a technical report, apparently in late October 2026, proposing EU-wide end-of-waste (EoW) criteria for preparing textile, textile-related and footwear waste for reuse and remanufacturing. It is the first time EU-wide EoW criteria are being developed for preparation for reuse. A companion report (JRC147954) covers mechanical, thermo-mechanical, physical and chemical recycling. These are proposals, not adopted law: a Commission implementing act is still needed, and no timeline has been found.

BackgroundThe revised Waste Framework Directive (EU 2025/1892) commits the Commission to an implementing act setting textile end-of-waste criteria based on the JRC's work. EU frameworks have so far focused mainly on recycling. Member states currently apply their own national EoW provisions, which the report reviews comparatively. The JRC report covers the sorting, preparing-for-reuse and remanufacturing phases.
ProblemWithout harmonised criteria, operators cannot be sure when a used garment legally stops being 'waste' and can be sold as a product. That uncertainty affects licensing, cross-border shipment and liability. The open questions are where the legal line is drawn and how strict the sorting and quality conditions are, because that determines which reuse and remanufacturing business models are viable.
Where & whyThe friction is at the sorting and grading node of the textile reverse supply chain, where material changes legal status. Regulatory fragmentation across member states creates uncertainty and compliance costs and limits scale and cross-border trade in sorted secondhand textiles. Harmonisation could lower those barriers, but strict criteria could raise sorting costs.
Stakeholders
Textile sorters and waste-management operators — Want clear, workable criteria that let sorted goods exit waste status without heavy compliance costs.
Reuse platforms and remanufacturers — Gain legal certainty and cross-border market access for recovered products.
Fashion brands under upcoming textile EPR — Collection and reuse targets become easier to meet if more collected items can legally re-enter markets.
European Commission and member-state regulators — Want to replace fragmented national rules without weakening environmental safeguards.
Research hints
💡 Treat a future implementing act as a staggered natural experiment: member states with strict versus lenient national EoW rules face different-sized shocks.
💡 Reverse-logistics models where a legal status threshold, not just quality, determines the value of a returned item.
💡 Puzzle: how do reuse criteria and recycling criteria interact? Does making reuse easier divert higher-grade feedstock away from recyclers?
Data leadsEurostat waste generation and treatment statistics for textiles, UN Comtrade flows of worn clothing (HS 6309) by member state, and the JRC report's comparative table of national EoW provisions.
Keywordsreverse logistics · regulatory uncertainty · extended producer responsibility · secondary materials · recommerce
Related research
Fast Fashion, Charities, and the Circular Economy: Challenges for Operations Management (Production and Operations Management, 2021)
Lays out operations challenges for textile reuse involving fast-fashion take-back, charities and EPR, the exact chain these EoW criteria would regulate.

04 · ESG & Disclosure

Google DeepMind, WRI and IIASA release ForTy 2020 global forest-type map; claimed accuracy beats the EU's EUDR baseline, but plantation detection is weak

Wood Central · Nov 01 · Read · ☆ Star

Google DeepMind and Google Research, with the World Resources Institute and IIASA, released Forest Typology (ForTy) 2020 around October 22, 2026. The map classifies every 10x10m patch of land into six forest and land-use types, separating natural forest and timber plantations from tree crops such as oil palm and rubber. On 8,190 expert-checked sites, the developers report 95.5% accuracy for forest versus non-forest, compared with 93.6% for the JRC's EUDR map, and 90.9% versus 87.8% for forest types. Other coverage reports the map identifies only about half of planted forests and tree crops and is wrong for about a third of areas it labels planted forest.

BackgroundUnder the EU Deforestation Regulation, wood cannot come from primary forest converted to plantation or planted forest after 2020, and tree crops are treated as farmland. Buyers therefore need each plot's land-use history. ForTy follows the partners' 2025 Natural Forests of the World map. Wood Central cites an August 2025 review finding that none of 21 public global maps could distinguish forest from tree crops and agroforestry. The accuracy figures are the developers' own evaluation, with no independent validation seen, and the licence terms were not checked.
ProblemEUDR due diligence depends on geospatial baselines, and competing maps disagree. The new map is reported to be better on aggregate accuracy but weakest on exactly the classes that matter most, planted forest and tree crops. Firms, auditors and authorities must decide which map to trust. Errors translate into falsely flagging compliant suppliers or clearing non-compliant ones.
Where & whyThe friction is at supplier screening and plot-level verification, where buyers face information asymmetry about upstream land history. Map choice becomes a de facto compliance standard. Free, better maps can cut due-diligence costs, but classification error rates that differ by class and region can shift compliance burdens onto particular origins and smallholders.
Stakeholders
EU importers of timber, palm, rubber and other EUDR commodities — Want cheap, defensible screening tools that reduce compliance costs and false positives.
European Commission / JRC — Its own baseline map is now benchmarked against a private alternative, which raises questions about the official reference.
Producers and smallholders in sourcing countries — Misclassification could exclude them from EU markets or force costly proof of compliance.
Google DeepMind, WRI, IIASA — Want adoption of their open data as the de facto deforestation baseline.
Research hints
💡 Information-asymmetry and screening models: how does a buyer's choice of classifier, with class-specific error rates, change which suppliers get dropped?
💡 Compare map disagreement across sourcing regions as a source of variation in effective compliance burden, a possible lens on supplier exit or trade diversion.
💡 Puzzle: aggregate accuracy improves while accuracy on the regulation-relevant classes stays poor. What metric should regulators actually use to accept a map?
Data leadsForTy 2020 and the JRC Global Forest Cover 2020 map overlaid with UN Comtrade EU imports of HS 44 (wood), 1511 (palm oil) and 4001 (rubber) by origin; Global Forest Watch tree-cover loss as a cross-check.
Keywordsdeforestation · multi-tier traceability · sourcing restrictions · supplier region risk · smallholder inclusion
Related research
Toward “Omniscient” supply chains: Enabling and incentivizing sustainable operations (Production and Operations Management, 2026)
Discusses how better visibility across value chains could enable and incentivize sustainability due diligence, directly relevant to geospatial screening tools for EUDR.
05 · Supply Chain Resilience

Stepped-up US cabotage enforcement removes Mexican drivers and tightens cross-border trucking capacity, Knight-Swift CEO says

FreightWaves · Nov 01 · Read · ☆ Star

Knight-Swift CEO Adam Miller told FreightWaves that enforcement of US cabotage restrictions 'is really ramping up' and could further tighten cross-border trucking capacity. He said drivers have lost visas over violations committed at previous carriers. Industry leaders estimated about five months earlier that around 3,200 Mexican drivers had been removed from cross-border operations. Separately, Nuevo León cut a state toll on trucks heading to the Colombia-Solidarity International Bridge by 50%, and Walmart plans a $400M distribution center near San Antonio with 425 jobs (other details unverified).

BackgroundCabotage rules bar foreign carriers and drivers from hauling domestic US loads, which limits how Mexican drivers can be used once they are in the US. Knight-Swift runs more than 26,000 tractors and owns TransMex, whose Mexico fleet runs over 1,100 tractor-trailers. Miller expects Mexico to 'play a bigger role' long term but calls tariffs 'a big unknown'. The Nuevo León toll, introduced April 14, 2025, is charged about 500 meters before the existing bridge tollbooth. The cut does not affect Laredo, Texas tolls, and its start date was not stated.
ProblemNearshoring raises demand for cross-border freight just as enforcement removes drivers from the pool. Carriers face visa risk from drivers' past conduct at other firms, which complicates hiring and staffing. Shippers investing in border-adjacent distribution face uncertain cross-border capacity and costs. The capacity impact is currently an executive's view, not official data.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the border handoff in US-Mexico trucking, where regulatory rules decide who may haul what and where. A sudden enforcement shock to labor supply interacts with fixed border infrastructure and local fees such as state tolls. Carriers also cannot fully observe drivers' compliance histories, so enforcement risk spills over across firms.
Stakeholders
Mexican cross-border drivers — Visas and livelihoods at risk, sometimes for violations at earlier employers.
Cross-border carriers (Knight-Swift/TransMex and others) — Shrinking driver pool, compliance costs and capacity constraints, alongside a growing long-run market.
US enforcement agencies — Want to uphold cabotage rules and protect domestic trucking markets.
Shippers and retailers siting near the border (e.g., Walmart's planned San Antonio DC) — Need reliable, affordable cross-border capacity for nearshored flows.
Nuevo León state government — Wants to keep freight routed through its bridges; the toll cut trades revenue for traffic.
Research hints
💡 Treat the cabotage crackdown as a labor-supply shock and look for effects on border crossing volumes, dwell times and cross-border rates versus domestic lanes.
💡 The Nuevo León toll cut is a small price shock at one bridge; route-choice and substitution across Laredo-area bridges could be a clean comparison.
💡 Puzzle: carriers bear enforcement risk from drivers' past behavior at other firms, an adverse-selection twist on driver screening.
Data leadsBTS border crossing entry data (truck crossings by port, monthly), BTS TransBorder freight by port and mode; DAT/SONAR cross-border rates if available; FMCSA carrier census for Mexico-domiciled carriers.
Keywordscross-border trucking · regulatory enforcement · driver supply shock · nearshoring logistics · freight rates
