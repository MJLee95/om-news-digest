Today's issue spans grid expansion for data centers, strategic stockpiling of oil and critical minerals, a national food-waste program running at half capacity, and Brazil's timber trade as the EU deforestation rule approaches. Several items share one question: who holds buffer capacity, where, and who pays for it.

01 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Northern Virginia's data center load drives 765-kV transmission lines across three states, prompting siting fights far from the demand

CNN · Oct 25 · Read · ☆ Star

CNN reported on Oct. 25, 2026 that new high-voltage lines are planned to address a steep power shortage in Northern Virginia, home to the world's largest concentration of data centers. The main project, Valley Link, is a 765-kV line on 160-foot towers running 260 miles from West Virginia through Virginia into Maryland, with a right-of-way as wide as a 16-lane highway. A second Valley Link segment would run 116 miles from rural central Virginia into Northern Virginia, and together the lines could cross dozens of counties home to more than 1.5 million people.

BackgroundData center demand is concentrated in a few load pockets, especially Loudoun County. Generation and transmission must reach them from elsewhere. Long-distance, high-voltage lines are the bulk-system answer, but their routes pass through rural communities that receive little of the load's economic benefit. A similar 2025 fight over the 67-mile Maryland Piedmont Reliability Project was also framed as an 'extension cord' for data centers.
ProblemResidents along the routes bear land-use, visual and property costs, while the benefits (power for data centers, tax base) accrue mainly to Loudoun County. Advocates, legal experts and even local officials told CNN residents have almost no means to stop the lines. Per analysts and lobbyists cited by CNN, a federal commission (likely FERC) has historically deferred to power operators' assessments of what transmission is needed. The contested decisions are how much transmission to build, where to route it, and how to allocate its costs and burdens.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the transmission planning and siting stage, between a geographically concentrated new load and dispersed host communities. Several forces drive it. Benefits and burdens are spatially mismatched. Need assessments rely on operators' load forecasts, an information asymmetry. Regulators tend to defer to those forecasts. Federal, state and local authorities overlap, so affected parties have weak veto power. Large-load demand forecasts are uncertain, which creates a risk of over- or under-building long-lived assets.
Stakeholders
Rural landowners along the routes (e.g., West Virginia property owners near the Shenandoah River) — Bear right-of-way, land-use and amenity costs with little direct benefit, and want the routes changed or stopped
Loudoun County data center operators — Need firm, timely power to grow capacity, and benefit from the new transmission
Transmission developers and grid operators — Want approval and cost recovery for projects justified by load-growth forecasts
Federal regulator (likely FERC) and state siting authorities — Must weigh reliability needs against local opposition and decide how costs are allocated
Ratepayers across the region — May pay for transmission built mainly to serve one customer class
Research hints
💡 Capacity expansion under demand uncertainty with lumpy, irreversible assets: compare the value of committing early to long lines with a staged or flexible build.
💡 Cost-allocation lens: compare 'beneficiary pays' with socialized regional allocation when the load is spatially concentrated.
💡 Puzzle: if local actors cannot block lines, do pushback and delay show up elsewhere, for example in docket duration, route changes or settlement terms?
Data leadsFERC eLibrary transmission filings and state PUC certificate dockets for line need and routing; PJM planning documents and capacity data; EIA-861 for utility sales growth in the affected territories.

02 · Supply Chain Resilience

Saudi Arabia plans to store crude in Singapore's Jurong Rock Caverns from H2 2027 as the Hormuz disruption hits Asian supply

The Economic Times · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

On Oct. 26, 2026, Singapore's energy minister Tan See Leng and Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman announced a 'joint intent to establish an arrangement' for Saudi crude storage at Singapore's Jurong Rock Caverns starting in the second half of 2027. The caverns lie 150 m underground and hold up to 1.47 million cubic meters (about 9 million barrels). The volume, pricing, and whether Singapore gets first right to buy in a shortage were not disclosed.

BackgroundThe US-Israeli war with Iran has badly disrupted shipments to Asia through the Strait of Hormuz, and countries have been drawing down stockpiles. Before the war, Asia got two-thirds of its oil from the Middle East. Aramco already stores crude in Japan and South Korea, where those governments hold first right to buy in a shortage. Okinawa storage was expanded to 8.2 million barrels in 2017 under a deal with JOGMEC, and a 2023 deal with Korea National Oil Corp covers 5.3 million barrels at Ulsan for five years.
ProblemA producer is choosing to pre-position inventory downstream, near customers, rather than hold it at origin behind a chokepoint. Open questions include how much to store, who owns and controls release, and how priority rights in a shortage are priced or traded off against the producer's commercial flexibility. The arrangement is only a statement of intent, so its final terms are uncertain.
Where & whyThe problem arises in the midstream link between Gulf production and Asian refiners, where one maritime chokepoint creates correlated disruption risk. Holding inventory near demand shortens response lead time and hedges the transit risk. The producer and host country have partly misaligned incentives: the host wants guaranteed access in a crisis, while the seller wants to keep the option to sell to the highest bidder.
Stakeholders
Saudi Arabia / Aramco — Wants to protect Asian market share and reliability reputation, and to keep commercial control over stored barrels
Singapore government — Wants better supply security and to strengthen its role as a regional hub; may seek first-purchase rights
Asian refiners and importers — Benefit from shorter replenishment lead times during Hormuz disruptions
Japan and South Korea (JOGMEC, KNOC) — Existing hosts whose arrangements set precedent; regional competition over who holds Saudi buffer stock
Research hints
💡 Inventory pre-positioning and network design with a chokepoint: the trade-off between holding stock at origin and holding it near demand.
💡 Contract-design lens: treat host-country first-purchase rights as a call option and ask how they should be priced against storage fees.
💡 Compare the terms of the Japan, Korea and Singapore arrangements as a small-sample look at how producer storage deals vary with host bargaining power.
Data leadsUN Comtrade crude flows by origin and destination; JODI oil stock data for Asian importers; AIS-based tanker tracking for flows through Hormuz and into Singapore.
Keywordschokepoint disruption · inventory management · long-term supply contracts · strategic stockpiling
Related research
Collaborative Prepositioning Network Design for Regional Disaster Response (Production and Operations Management, 2019)
Designs collaborative prepositioning networks in which countries pool stocks across correlated disruption risks, a close analogue to producer-host storage arrangements in Asia.
Preparedness with a system integrating inventory, capacity, and capability for future pandemics and other disasters (Production and Operations Management, 2022)
Models how stockpile inventory combines with backup capacity for surge demand, useful for thinking about how much stored crude a disruption-prone importer needs.
03 · Critical Minerals & Batteries

European Parliament backs JOGMEC-style agency that could take equity stakes and stockpile critical minerals

Financial Times · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

According to the FT, as relayed in a secondhand rewrite, European Parliament lawmakers voted to propose a Critical Raw Materials Centre. The agency could buy stakes in raw materials companies, build EU stockpiles and track supplies across the bloc. It would get €2bn under the next long-term EU budget, an amount its sponsor, MEP Mohammed Chahim, concedes would need private, national and EIB co-funding to succeed. The model is Japan's JOGMEC, and the push follows US moves such as the Pentagon's $400mn, 15% stake in MP Materials with a 10-year $110/kg NdPr price floor.

BackgroundThe EU depends heavily on Chinese critical minerals, and its existing approach under the Critical Raw Materials Act has been faster permitting for strategic projects without guaranteed public money. This month 46 projects were added to the existing 60. The Commission dropped its plan to propose legislation for a centre this autumn, so Chahim inserted the idea as amendments to the revision of the Critical Raw Materials Act.
ProblemShould the EU move from permitting support to direct state participation (equity stakes, stockpiles and possibly price support) to secure supply? The trade-offs are cost against effectiveness: €2bn alone is seen as too small. There is also a risk of swapping one dependency for another; EIT RawMaterials' Bernd Schäfer warns Europe could become as dependent on US rare earths as it is on China. Member states, including previously sceptical Germany and Spain, must still approve, so the outcome is uncertain.
Where & whyThe friction is at the upstream mining and processing stage. Concentrated foreign supply and price swings that foreign players can manipulate deter private investment in European projects, a market failure from price uncertainty and strategic behavior by dominant suppliers. Coordinating 27 member states and pooling funding adds a collective-action problem.
Stakeholders
European Parliament (MEP Mohammed Chahim) — Wants an agency to cut dependence on China and shield EU projects from price manipulation
Member states (e.g., Germany, Spain) — Must approve the agency and equity stakes; weigh fiscal cost and sovereignty
European Commission — Shelved its own centre proposal; now faces it via the CRMA revision
EU mining and processing project developers — Could gain equity, offtake and price stability, beyond permitting speed alone
Downstream manufacturers (e.g., magnet users) — Want secure, affordable supply without a new single-country dependency
Research hints
💡 Compare instruments: equity stakes, price floors, stockpiles and permitting fast-tracks as alternative ways to de-risk supply; contrast the MP Materials deal with the JOGMEC model.
💡 Strategic stockpile design under a dominant, possibly manipulative supplier, including how stockpile size interacts with the supplier's pricing behavior.
💡 Natural-experiment angle: compare strategic-project firms with and without later public financing once the centre's status is settled.
Data leadsUSGS Mineral Commodity Summaries and UN Comtrade for EU import concentration by mineral; the EU CRMA strategic projects list for project-level outcomes; SEC EDGAR filings for MP Materials deal terms.
Keywordsgovernment equity stakes · raw material security · price floors · industrial policy · supplier concentration
Related research
Building Supply Chain Resilience through Virtual Stockpile Pooling (Production and Operations Management, 2016)
Proposes virtual stockpile pooling to cut the holding costs of resilience stockpiles, directly relevant to how an EU-level centre might structure shared mineral reserves.
04 · Circular Economy

Türkiye's food-waste-to-animal-feed program covers 74 of 81 provinces but runs at under half its capacity

Hürriyet Daily News · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

Interior Ministry sources say food waste from public institutions and large catering sites is converted into animal feed in 74 of Türkiye's 81 provinces, with the feed going to stray animals, shelters and designated habitats rather than livestock. Combined daily capacity is 65,352 kg, but actual output is 31,274 kg a day. Seven provinces (Iğdır, Kahramanmaraş, Kırıkkale, Kocaeli, Kütahya, Samsun, Tunceli) have no equipment.

BackgroundThe program is part of the Zero Waste Project led by first lady Emine Erdoğan. Governors' offices and municipalities collect waste from KYK student dormitories, military units, hospitals, prisons, hotels and restaurants. An inspection report recommends also sending food unfit for people but fine for animals to shelters and habitats, including food ordered destroyed under insurance procedures or official inspections. It also calls for more processing equipment at shelters. Interior Minister Mustafa Çiftçi aims to build plants in the seven missing provinces. No timeline, cost or start date was given.
ProblemCapacity is in place but underused, with output under half of installed capacity, while the government plans to expand into more provinces. The open question is whether the bottleneck is supply of waste, collection logistics, demand at shelters, or plant location. The proposal to divert condemned or insurance-destroyed food also raises safety and quality-control questions.
Where & whyThe gap sits in the reverse-logistics link between dispersed institutional waste generators and processing plants, and between plants and end users at shelters and habitats. Likely frictions include mismatches between waste sources and plant locations, collection costs, uneven waste volumes, and coordination across ministries, municipalities and private hotels and restaurants. Food-safety screening of condemned food adds a regulatory constraint.
Stakeholders
Interior Ministry and provincial governors — Want nationwide coverage across all 81 provinces and a sustainable system
Municipalities — Run collection and may save on landfill, but bear logistics costs
Institutional waste generators (dorms, hospitals, military, prisons, hotels) — Need reliable pickup and clear rules on what waste qualifies
Animal shelters and habitat managers — Receive feed and want safe, consistent quality and on-site processing equipment
Research hints
💡 Facility location and capacity sizing for reverse logistics: why might a network sit at about 48% utilization, and is adding plants in new provinces the right next move?
💡 Compare institutional sources (dorms, hospitals, hotels) on waste volume predictability and contamination risk.
💡 Food-safety and biosecurity screening as a constraint when diverting condemned or insurance-destroyed food into feed.
Data leadsProvince-level capacity and output figures from the Interior Ministry and Zero Waste Project reports; TurkStat municipal waste statistics.
Keywordsfood waste valorization · reverse logistics · capacity utilization · zero waste certification · biosecurity risk
05 · ESG & Disclosure

Lula wins fourth term as Brazil's timber exports face EU deforestation-rule checks

Wood Central · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

Lula won Brazil's Oct. 25, 2026 runoff with 51.79% to Flávio Bolsonaro's 48.21%, per the Superior Electoral Court, after trailing in the first round, 47% to 45% per AP. Wood Central reports Amazon deforestation is down 71.2% over four years, the lowest since satellite records began in 1988. It also reports the EU's checks on Brazilian wood under the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) start at the end of the year. Bolsonaro has reportedly refused to concede, and legal challenges over a court-ordered two-hour voting extension are possible.

BackgroundThe EUDR requires due diligence and traceability showing that commodities such as timber entering the EU are not linked to deforestation. Pará and Mato Grosso remain the biggest sources of Amazon deforestation. In his victory speech, Lula praised his environment ministry leaders Marina Silva and João Paulo Capobianco and repeated the goal of zero deforestation by 2030. Wood Central notes the Lula government is one 'Washington faulted over illegal deforestation.' The source of the deforestation figure and the exact EUDR start date are not confirmed here.
ProblemBrazilian timber exporters must show plot-level traceability to keep EU market access, just as political continuity, contested by the losing side, shapes domestic enforcement. EU buyers can invest in verifying Brazilian suppliers or exclude higher-risk regions and suppliers. Strong national deforestation statistics may not translate into supplier-level compliance, especially in the high-deforestation states.
Where & whyThe friction is at the upstream sourcing and verification link between forest origins in states like Pará and Mato Grosso, intermediaries, and EU importers. It stems from information asymmetry about origin, the cost of multi-tier traceability, and regulatory uncertainty from timing, enforcement and the political transition. Buyers face incentives to de-risk by excluding suppliers rather than upgrading them.
Stakeholders
Brazilian timber exporters and smallholders — Risk losing EU market access if they cannot meet traceability requirements
EU importers and operators — Face due-diligence liability and may shift sourcing away from high-risk origins
Lula government / environment ministry — Wants deforestation credibility and zero deforestation by 2030 to support trade access
Bolsonaro camp — Contesting the result, which adds political and enforcement uncertainty
Research hints
💡 Supplier exclusion versus supplier development under due-diligence regulation: which do buyers choose, and how does regional risk shape the choice?
💡 Event-study or difference-in-differences framing around EUDR enforcement dates, comparing EU-bound with non-EU-bound Brazilian timber flows.
💡 Puzzle: does improving aggregate deforestation reduce firm-level compliance costs, or do the costs stay concentrated in high-risk states?
Data leadsUN Comtrade Brazilian wood exports by destination; INPE PRODES deforestation by state and municipality; Trase commodity supply chain mappings.
Keywordsdeforestation · multi-tier traceability · regulatory enforcement · sourcing restrictions · regulatory uncertainty
Related research
Sustainability in multi‐tier supply chains: Understanding the double agency role of the first‐tier supplier (Journal of Operations Management, 2015)
Examines when first-tier suppliers act as agents enforcing sustainability compliance further upstream, central to how EUDR due diligence would flow through Brazilian timber intermediaries.
Toward “Omniscient” supply chains: Enabling and incentivizing sustainable operations (Production and Operations Management, 2026)
Discusses enabling and incentivizing value-chain-wide sustainability due diligence and the limits of market and regulatory enforcement, a direct frame for EUDR traceability.
Thread to pull

Japan's JOGMEC appears twice today: as the Okinawa host of Saudi crude storage and as the explicit model for the EU's proposed Critical Raw Materials Centre. Comparing producer-placed storage near demand (Saudi crude in Singapore) with buyer-owned state stockpiles and equity stakes (the EU minerals plan) could clarify who should own buffer inventory under chokepoint or supplier-concentration risk, and how first-purchase rights or price floors split that risk.
