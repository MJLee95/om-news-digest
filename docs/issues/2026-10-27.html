Tuesday, October 27, 2026

Two hourly clean-power rules are moving in different directions. GHG Protocol is delaying hourly matching for corporate Scope 2 claims, while Spain keeps an 80% hourly renewable requirement for data centers even as it loosens other parts of its draft. Also in this issue: Anthropic's S-1 puts numbers on AI compute contracting, Mexican truckers strike on nearshoring corridors, and Shaw runs carpet take-back on empty backhauls.

01 · ESG & Disclosure

GHG Protocol proposes delaying mandatory hourly matching and deliverability in Scope 2 accounting after weak corporate support

ESG Today · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

GHG Protocol is revising its Scope 2 standard. It now proposes that companies "should" use a stricter Method 1, which requires hourly matching and deliverability, but "may" keep current rules (Method 2) until an unspecified future date, and must disclose which method they use. In the consultation, only 12% of companies supported hourly matching and 19% supported deliverability. A new consultation is planned for Q2 2027, feeding a joint GHG Protocol–ISO Corporate Standard expected in Q4 2028.

BackgroundScope 2 covers emissions from purchased electricity, steam, heat and cooling. Under the 2015 market-based method, firms can claim renewable purchases matched annually and across wide geographies. That is what lets many firms report near-zero Scope 2 emissions. A technical working group began the revision in 2024, and public consultation opened in October 2025. Hourly matching would require clean-energy contracts to match consumption hour by hour. Deliverability would require the power to come from the same electricity market where it is used.
ProblemThe standard-setter must trade off accuracy against participation. Stricter rules would make renewable claims reflect physical grid impact more closely. But companies warned of these costs: - Hourly matching could discourage participation in voluntary clean-energy markets (87%). - Hourly matching would add reporting burden and cost (86%). - Deliverability would narrow where firms can invest (87%). - Deliverability could push buyers from long-term contracts toward spot purchases (72%). The compromise leaves the mandatory date open. Method 1 also becomes required only if data meeting quality criteria exists, which could delay it further.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the measurement rule that turns procurement contracts into reported emissions. - Firms' incentives are misaligned with the standard's accuracy goal: cheaper annual certificates yield the same claimed reduction. - Hourly data is often missing, so measurement is costly and depends on data availability. - The standard is set by consensus among the very firms it governs, which gives them leverage. - The undefined deadline creates regulatory uncertainty for long-dated PPA decisions.
Stakeholders
Large corporate electricity buyers — Keep low-cost annual-matching claims and avoid compliance cost. Some may lose credibility if their claims are seen as weak.
GHG Protocol / Independent Standard Board — Balance scientific integrity against broad adoption, and keep alignment with the upcoming ISO standard.
Renewable developers and PPA sellers — Contract demand, pricing, and the value of firmed or storage-backed supply depend on which rule wins.
Investors and ESG raters — Need comparable Scope 2 figures. During the transition, two methods will coexist under a disclosure flag.
Research hints
💡 Disclosing which method a firm uses creates a self-selection signal. Which firms voluntarily adopt Method 1 early, and does early adoption correlate with existing 24/7 procurement or grid mix?
💡 Contract-portfolio models of corporate renewable targets (VPPAs under uncertainty) could be extended to ask how hourly constraints change the optimal mix of long-term versus spot procurement.
💡 Consultation responses are a form of corporate lobbying. Comparing who objected with firms' location-based versus market-based Scope 2 gap is one angle.
Data leadsCDP climate responses (location- vs market-based Scope 2, instruments used), SBTi dashboard, EIA-930 hourly grid data to build counterfactual hourly-matched emissions, and SEC EDGAR filings for PPA disclosures.
Keywordsmarket-based emissions accounting · sustainability standards · corporate lobbying · power purchase agreements · voluntary disclosure

02 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Anthropic's S-1 discloses roughly $265bn in fixed compute commitments and $417bn in new lease and capacity agreements

DatacenterDynamics · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

Anthropic filed a 335-page S-1 to list on Nasdaq as ANT. DCD reports three separate commitment figures: - About $265.6bn in fixed or minimum commitments, with 1–20 year terms. - About $417bn in future minimum payments under new or amended leases and commercial agreements, mostly for compute. These start in 2026–27 and run up to 20 years. - An estimated $125–150bn for capacity delivered in 2028–2031. In H1 2026, revenue rose 1,300% to $16.3bn. The net loss was $80.7bn, of which $72.4bn was a one-off charge, and the operating loss was $693m. (Disclosure: this newsletter is drafted with Claude, an Anthropic model. Figures come from DCD and have not been checked against the filing.)

BackgroundAI developers secure compute through long-dated leases of data centers and equipment and through purchase commitments with cloud providers. Named suppliers include Google, AWS, Microsoft, SpaceXAI, Fluidstack, Nscale, Akamai and Lambda. Deals described in the filing: - A 20-year lease on a data center not yet built. - Compute rental paid with up to $42bn in convertible notes. - A $500m power purchase contract tied to a data center provider deal. - A $2.5bn escrow deposit for a compute lease. In the June 2026 quarter, compute and infrastructure costs grew 300% against 1,368% revenue growth. DCD dates the $417bn figure "as of December 2025" even though the commitments start in 2026–27, which is worth checking in the S-1.
ProblemThe firm is locking in decades of fixed capacity while demand is growing fast but highly uncertain. Some agreements have no fixed amount, but the filing says payments are "expected to be significant." This raises three linked questions: - How much capacity risk is shifted to suppliers, and how much to Anthropic and its future shareholders? - How are payments structured (escrow, convertible notes)? - What power, water and climate exposure comes with the capacity?
Where & whyThe friction sits in the upstream capacity market linking AI labs, neocloud and hyperscale providers, and grid or power suppliers. - Long build lead times and scarce power force commitments years before demand is known. This is classic capacity investment under uncertainty with take-or-pay contracts. - Suppliers want guaranteed volumes to finance construction. - The buyer wants flexibility, but has to secure scarce capacity before competitors do.
Stakeholders
Anthropic and prospective IPO investors — Secure enough compute for growth. Fixed obligations create downside risk if demand or pricing disappoints.
Cloud and neocloud providers (Google, AWS, Microsoft, Fluidstack, Nscale, Lambda, etc.) — Long-term commitments underwrite their capex. They face counterparty concentration risk.
Data center developers and power suppliers — 20-year leases and PPAs justify new builds. Grid access and timing are the binding constraints.
Utilities and local communities — Absorb new load, water use and grid-upgrade costs tied to these commitments.
Research hints
💡 Capacity reservation contracts with transfer rights are a lens here. What happens when a buyer over-reserves compute and can resell, or can't?
💡 S-1 and 10-K commitment tables (contractual obligations, lease maturities) across AI firms and hyperscalers could be compared to map who bears capacity risk along the chain.
💡 The ratio of compute-cost growth to revenue growth is a quantified scaling signal worth tracking across filings.
Data leadsThe Anthropic S-1 and supplier 10-Ks/8-Ks on SEC EDGAR (purchase obligations, lease footnotes, risk factors), plus earnings call transcripts of named cloud providers; LBNL queue data for the power side.
Keywordscapacity investment under uncertainty · long-term supply contracts · data center electricity demand · supplier concentration · capital expenditure
Related research
Should suppliers allow capacity transfers? (Production and Operations Management, 2022)
Studies capacity reservation where buyers pre-pay for supplier capacity and whether excess can be transferred, directly analogous to long-dated compute reservations.
03 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Spain softens draft data center decree: 10 MW threshold, 36-month matching window, but 80% hourly renewable coverage stays

Reuters · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

On October 26, 2026, Spain's energy ministry released a more flexible draft decree on data centers. Changes reported by Reuters: - The rules now apply only above 10 MW, up from 1 MW. - New renewable capacity matching each new megawatt can now be built within 36 months before operation, up from 18. Solar has no time limit. - Renewables must still cover at least 80% of energy in every operating hour. - A new alternative path allows up to five years to reach 80%, under conditions. Bloomberg reports a 60% renewable requirement in peak (P1) hours until 2029.

BackgroundSpain has become one of Europe's hottest data center hubs, attracting AI infrastructure projects worth tens of billions of euros. A wave of project announcements preceded the revision. Bloomberg framed the change as a response to strong industry pushback against the first draft. The decree still pairs new data center load with additionality: new renewables built near in time to the load. Note this text comes from syndicated Reuters copies; the original Reuters article was not verified.
ProblemSpain wants to keep attracting investment while ensuring new load is met by new clean supply. The first draft was seen as too strict. - The higher threshold exempts smaller facilities. - The longer window and transition path weaken short-run additionality. - The 80% hourly floor still imposes costly firming, such as storage or overbuilding. It is unclear how Bloomberg's 60%-until-2029 figure relates to the five-year transition. The full conditions and adoption timing are not reported.
Where & whyThe friction sits at siting and interconnection for large loads. - Regulators fear that concentrated new demand will raise prices or emissions. Developers face hourly coverage constraints that solar alone cannot meet at night. - Thresholds and windows create sharp regulatory cutoffs that can distort project size and timing. - Regulators and investors compete across jurisdictions, which pushes rules toward leniency.
Stakeholders
Spanish energy ministry — Capture data center investment without straining the grid or undermining decarbonization goals.
Hyperscalers and data center developers — Lower compliance cost and faster permitting. Hourly coverage still raises capex for storage or overbuilt renewables.
Renewable and storage developers — Matching requirements create demand for new solar, wind and firming capacity tied to data center offtake.
Other electricity consumers — Exposure to price and reliability effects of large new loads.
Research hints
💡 The jump from 1 MW to 10 MW is a regulatory threshold. Watch for bunching of announced project sizes just below 10 MW, a classic notch design.
💡 Compare Spain with jurisdictions using other large-load rules (moratoria, special tariffs, Scope 2-style hourly matching) as a siting-choice problem.
💡 An 80% hourly floor creates a portfolio design problem: what mix of solar, wind and storage meets the floor at least cost, and how does the solar exemption from the time limit change it?
Data leadsRed Eléctrica (REE) and ENTSO-E hourly generation and demand data, Spanish BOE publication of the decree versions, and project announcement trackers for data center pipelines in Spain.
Keywordsregulatory thresholds · facility siting · data center electricity demand · large-load tariffs · corporate lobbying
Related research
The Unintended Carbon Impacts of Large-Scale Electricity Storage (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2025)
Shows how storage arbitrage changes market-level emissions indirectly, relevant to whether storage used to satisfy an hourly 80% rule actually cuts emissions.
04 · Supply Chain Resilience

CANACAR launches indefinite nationwide Mexican trucking strike over cargo theft, violence and toll tax credit changes

FreightWaves · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

On Monday, October 26, 2026, Mexico's freight chamber CANACAR and other trucking groups began an indefinite nationwide strike. Protests were planned in Nuevo Laredo, Reynosa, Monterrey, Tijuana and Mexico City. The Interior Ministry urged talks and said several other transport groups had agreed to negotiate, but CANACAR kept its protest call. Strike duration, participation and border impact were unknown as of Monday.

BackgroundNuevo Laredo sits across from Laredo, Texas, the top US inland port of entry, so it is central to US–Mexico nearshoring flows. CANACAR president Augusto Ramos Melo listed these grievances: - cargo theft - violence against drivers - deteriorating highways - excessive towing fees - regulatory burdens Extortion is also cited. The groups also oppose a change in Mexico's 2027 fiscal package that would narrow eligibility for a tax credit on some highway toll costs. Planned tactics included convoys and trucks parked along highways and near logistics facilities.
ProblemCarriers are using collective disruption to push the government on security and cost. Shippers and US buyers relying on Mexican production face possible delays at key freight points. - The strike is indefinite. - The ministry is negotiating with some transport groups but not CANACAR. - No figures on trucks involved, delays or economic cost exist yet.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the domestic Mexican trucking link that feeds border crossings. - Insecurity (theft, extortion) and fiscal changes raise carriers' costs. - Carriers can't easily pass these costs on, so they turn to collective action. - This coordination problem between government and fragmented carrier groups becomes a supply shock for cross-border supply chains concentrated at a few crossings.
Stakeholders
CANACAR and Mexican carriers — Better highway security, lower towing and regulatory costs, and keeping the toll tax credit.
Mexican government (Interior Ministry, fiscal authorities) — End disruption without conceding fiscal revenue; split negotiating groups.
US and Mexican manufacturers and shippers — Delivery delays and inventory disruption on nearshored supply chains.
US carriers and brokers at Laredo and other ports — Volume swings, rate effects and capacity rebalancing at the border.
Research hints
💡 Event-study the strike against daily or monthly border crossing counts at Laredo versus less affected ports. Its start date is sharp, but the end date and intensity are uncertain.
💡 Chronic risks such as cargo theft and extortion versus acute strikes: does nearshoring risk show up as an inventory buffer, a route shift, or a mode shift to rail?
💡 Port concentration matters: how exposed are firms whose bills of lading flow almost entirely through Nuevo Laredo?
Data leadsBTS border crossing entry data (truck crossings by port, monthly), BTS TransBorder freight by mode and port, CBP wait-time feeds, and DAT/SONAR cross-border rates (commercial; BTS as a public alternative).
Keywordscross-border trucking · nearshoring logistics · chokepoint disruption · supplier region risk
Related research
IMPACT OF DISASTERS ON FIRMS IN DIFFERENT SECTORS: IMPLICATIONS FOR SUPPLY CHAINS (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2010)
Uses secondary data to estimate how disruptions affect firms across sectors, a template for measuring which industries a cross-border trucking shock hurts most.
05 · Circular Economy

Shaw Industries runs carpet tile take-back through its outbound distribution network using empty backhauls

FreightWaves · Oct 26 · Read · ☆ Star

Starting in 2022–23, Shaw routed used EcoWorx carpet tile returns through its regional distribution centers. Delivery trucks pick up tile, material from several jobs is combined, and it moves to northwest Georgia on backhauls that would otherwise return empty. In November 2023, the free-pickup minimum (re[TURN] program) fell from 500 to 100 square yards. Shaw now turns 100% of the tile into new backing and says it has reclaimed nearly 1 billion pounds of carpet since 2006.

BackgroundShaw's network: - 2,700 deliveries a day, reaching 99% of the US population within two days. - About 30 regional distribution centers as of 2023. - 689 tractors and 3,191 trailers, ranking 75th among US private carriers in 2026. Carpet tile comes up 7–10 years after installation, often in small batches. By then owners may have changed or may not know the maker. Dedicated return trucks only paid off with near-full loads. Pickup sites still need room for a 53-foot trailer, and third-party partners cover remote areas.
ProblemReturns are dispersed, delayed and small, so collection is costly. Shaw won't share its return rate and calls getting tile back "challenging." - Returns come most easily where landfill fees are high: California, New York, Massachusetts and Seattle. - They are harder in Alabama and Mississippi. - Demolition contractors control disposal and often dump everything together, so Shaw has hired specialists in Seattle, Boston and Dallas. The cost savings from the backhaul model are not quantified.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the end-of-life collection stage. - The decision-maker (the demolition contractor) is not the buyer or the manufacturer, so incentives are misaligned. - Local landfill fees set the outside option, which explains geographic variation. - Matching returns with forward freight lowers marginal transport cost, but trailer access constraints and small volumes limit coverage.
Stakeholders
Shaw Industries — Recover feedstock for new backing at low cost and support sustainability claims. It bears collection and outreach costs.
Demolition contractors — Want fast, cheap disposal. Sorting tile adds handling unless tipping fees make diversion cheaper.
Building owners and tenants — Possible disposal savings and green building credits, but may not know who made the tile.
State and local governments and landfill operators — Tipping fees and waste policy determine how much carpet is diverted.
Research hints
💡 Cross-state variation in landfill tipping fees is a natural lens for explaining return rates. Compare high-fee coastal markets with low-fee Southern states.
💡 Vehicle routing with pickups and deliveries (backhaul integration) is a model family for when lowering a minimum pickup size from 500 to 100 sq yd pays off.
💡 Return delays of 7–10 years and uncertain return quantity matter for planning recycled-content inputs in a closed loop.
Data leadsEPA materials, waste and recycling facts and figures (carpet and rug generation and landfill), state EPR reports including California's carpet stewardship program, and state/municipal tipping fee surveys.
Keywordsreverse logistics · closed-loop supply chain · extended producer responsibility · secondary materials
Related research
Managerial Efficiency and Reverse‐Flow Complexity in a Circular Economy (Journal of Operations Management, 2025)
Examines how delayed, complex reverse flows distort managerial decisions in circular systems, relevant to tile that returns 7–10 years after sale.
Closed‐loop supply chains with product remanufacturing: Challenges and opportunities (Journal of Operations Management, 2024)
Special-issue overview of pressure points in remanufacturing closed-loop supply chains, including collection, useful framing for Shaw's take-back.
Thread to pull

Hourly matching shows up in three items. GHG Protocol is postponing it for voluntary Scope 2 claims, Spain keeps an 80% hourly floor for data centers by regulation, and Anthropic's S-1 lists a $500m PPA attached to compute capacity. Where voluntary standards soften while national siting rules hard-code hourly coverage, data center buyers may face two different definitions of clean power at once. Comparing how the two constraints shape procurement and siting could be fruitful.
