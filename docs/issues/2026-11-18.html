01 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Orennia: 191 GW of planned US data center capacity at risk after midterms, exposing utility and generator investments

Utility Dive · Nov 17 · Read · ☆ Star

An Orennia report shared with Utility Dive (Nov. 17, 2026) estimates that about 191 GW of planned data center capacity faces new risk. The exposure is concentrated in Texas, Ohio and Georgia, where incoming Democratic governors campaigned on pausing or restricting construction. In Orennia's scenarios, if 20% of projected ERCOT data center demand never appears, average wholesale revenue falls 38% for solar and 37% for wind, and implied heat rates fall 39%. If half of PJM's planned buildout slips two years through 2030, four-hour battery spreads fall about 16%.

BackgroundUtilities and merchant developers have been sizing generation, storage and transmission around large-load forecasts driven by AI data centers. Texas holds nearly half of US data center capacity that is in development but not yet under construction. Over the summer, Gov. Greg Abbott paused new data center grid connections while the queue was audited. He then lost re-election to Gina Hinojosa, who has pledged to keep pressure on the industry. Bloomberg tracked 46 local ballot measures on data centers, and nearly all ended in restrictions, moratoriums or bans.
ProblemInfrastructure built for loads that are delayed, shrunk or cancelled may leave utilities and their ratepayers paying for billions in stranded or underused assets. The same demand shortfall would also depress wholesale prices and storage spreads below break-even for new projects. Industry signals conflict: CNBC reported that every utility CEO it spoke to at the EEI conference saw no slowdown in their demand-growth estimates. These are scenario models, not forecasts.
Where & whyThe problem sits at the link between load forecasting and capacity commitment. Long-lead generation, storage and grid investments must be committed before large-load demand is realised. Political and siting uncertainty now adds a regime-shift risk on top of ordinary forecast error. Information is asymmetric: developers know their true project pipelines, while utilities see queue requests. Dynamix Capital Partners' Andrejka Bernatova warns that prolonged uncertainty raises the cost of capital and makes investors hesitant to commit before power is secured.
Stakeholders
Utilities (e.g., in ERCOT, PJM, Georgia, Ohio) — Risk stranded or underused infrastructure built for data center load and disputes over who bears the cost.
Merchant solar, wind, battery and gas developers — Wholesale revenue, capture prices and storage spreads could fall below break-even if demand fails to appear.
Data center developers — Face approval freezes, moratoriums and higher capital costs in key states.
Incoming state governors and local voters — Want to control grid costs and local impacts, but restrictions could shift the national buildout elsewhere.
Residential and commercial ratepayers — May ultimately pay for capacity built for loads that never connect.
Research hints
💡 Capacity investment under demand uncertainty that can jump (a policy regime switch), not just drift: compare real-options value of waiting versus committing early.
💡 Treat Abbott's connection pause and post-election state policy changes as policy shocks; compare queue attrition across ERCOT, PJM and Georgia.
💡 Puzzle: utility CEOs report no slowdown while analysts model large risk. Whose forecasts are biased, and do regulated incentives (rate-base growth) play a role?
Data leadsERCOT large-load interconnection reports, LBNL Queued Up queue data, PJM load forecasts and capacity auction results, state PUC integrated resource plan dockets, EIA-860 planned generator additions.
Keywordsdata center electricity demand · capacity investment under uncertainty · siting moratorium · load forecasting · regulatory uncertainty
Related research

Models capacity investment when demand is learned over time, a natural frame for utilities deciding how much to build ahead of uncertain data center load.
Valuing supply‐chain responsiveness under demand jumps (Journal of Operations Management, 2018)
Shows how the value of responsiveness depends on whether forecasts evolve gradually or jump, relevant when political shocks suddenly shift expected data center demand.
02 · Supply Chain Resilience

Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd move three Gemini Asia–Europe services back to the Suez Canal from December

FreightWaves · Nov 17 · Read · ☆ Star

Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd will move three Gemini Cooperation Asia–Europe services back to the Suez Canal/Red Sea route instead of sailing around the Cape of Good Hope, in both directions. The first Suez sailings, by departure from the first loading port, are AE2/NE1 (Mary Maersk from Ningbo, Dec. 4), AE3/NE3 (Alette Maersk from Shanghai, Dec. 20) and AE1/NE2 (Damietta Express from Shanghai, Jan. 14, 2027). According to Linerlytica, Gemini will be the first alliance to route all of its Asia–Europe services through Suez.

BackgroundCarriers began avoiding the Red Sea in late 2023 because of Houthi attacks and are returning as attacks have eased. MSC has already moved all of its Asia–North Europe services back, and ONE and the Premier Alliance are also returning. Zencargo notes that Maersk-operated ships on these services have used Suez since October, so this is a phased network change rather than a single switch.
ProblemShorter voyages free up ships, but it is unclear how and where carriers will redeploy that capacity. Effects on transit times, schedule reliability, congestion and rates are uncertain. DHL Global Forwarding warns supply chains could stay under pressure into 2027. Zencargo says late-November Asia–Europe space is mostly full and rates have fallen only slightly. Maersk says the plan depends on the Red Sea staying stable.
Where & whyThe problem arises in carrier network design and at the chokepoint. Rerouting changes the number of vessels needed per weekly loop, so restoring Suez creates a temporary capacity surplus. It also creates bunching and port congestion as Cape and Suez sailings overlap. Security risk and a possible re-closure create real-option-like uncertainty for carriers. Shippers' inventory and lead-time buffers built for Cape transits must also be unwound.
Stakeholders
Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd (Gemini Cooperation) — Fuel and vessel savings and competitive transit times, weighed against security risk and the cost of reversing routes.
Competing carriers (MSC, ONE, Premier Alliance) — Network timing, market share and rate levels as freed-up capacity hits the market.
US and European shippers — Lower rates and shorter lead times, but exposure to congestion and redeployed capacity on other lanes.
European ports — Congestion as vessel arrival patterns shift during the transition.
Research hints
💡 Event study around the staggered service-level return dates (Dec. 4, Dec. 20, Jan. 14) to separate routing effects from market-wide trends.
💡 Look at the asymmetry between disruption onset and recovery: do schedule reliability, rates and shipper inventories normalise as fast as they deteriorated?
💡 Fleet redeployment as a capacity-allocation problem: where do freed Asia–Europe ships go, and do transpacific rates respond?
Data leadsAIS vessel tracks (NOAA MarineCadastre for US waters; global AIS for Suez transits), Sea-Intelligence schedule reliability and Xeneta rates, carrier service schedules; Panjiva bills of lading for shipper-side responses.
Keywordschokepoint disruption · freight rates · port congestion · slot sharing · war-risk insurance
Related research
What Options Do We Have? The Supply Chain Resilience Funnel (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2025)
Conceptualises supply chain resilience as evolving through adaptive cycles, useful for framing recovery after a two-year chokepoint disruption.
03 · Sustainable Operations

Nestlé tells dairy suppliers to meet decarbonisation standards or risk losing its business

https://en.edairynews.com/ · Nov 17 · Read · ☆ Star

According to eDairyNews (Nov. 17, 2026), which rewrites a BusinessDesk report, Nestlé chief procurement officer Jacob Busk Nielsen told dairy processors and cooperatives that missing its decarbonisation and regenerative-farming standards risks losing Nestlé as a buyer. Nestlé sources from more than 130,000 farms and over 200 suppliers. It reports dairy supply-chain GHG emissions down 26% and methane intensity down 25% from a 2018 baseline, toward a 2050 net-zero target.

BackgroundNestlé, with CHF 89.5 billion in annual revenue, is described as the world's largest buyer of dairy ingredients. Dairy is a large part of its Scope 3 emissions. It already works with partners including Fonterra, FrieslandCampina, Sodiaal, Lactalis, Land O'Lakes, Agropur and California Dairies. Programs include about 300 California farms, a Dutch target to halve milk's environmental footprint by 2030, and Brazil's Nature por NINHO, which improved feed efficiency by more than 34%.
ProblemA buyer is shifting from supplier development toward the threat of supplier exit to meet Scope 3 targets. This raises questions about credibility, verification of farm-level emissions, and cost-sharing with processors and farmers. No deadline or number of suppliers at risk was given. Stronger claims, such as 'progressive exclusion' from tenders and mandatory farm-level checks, may be eDairyNews's framing rather than Nestlé's words.
Where & whyThe friction sits in a multi-tier chain. Nestlé contracts with processors and cooperatives, but emissions occur on tens of thousands of farms it does not contract with directly. Emissions are hard to observe and verify at farm level, which creates information asymmetry. Incentives are also misaligned: abatement costs fall on farmers, while Scope 3 credit accrues to the buyer. First-tier processors must act as agents passing requirements upstream.
Stakeholders
Nestlé (procurement / Nestrade) — Meeting Scope 3 and net-zero commitments while keeping secure, cost-competitive dairy supply.
Dairy processors and cooperatives (e.g., Fonterra, FrieslandCampina, Lactalis) — Keeping a large customer, but bearing verification and programme costs and passing requirements to farms.
Dairy farmers — Abatement and regenerative-practice costs, and potential exclusion if their cooperative loses contracts.
Competing dairy buyers — May set competing or less stringent standards, shaping where non-compliant milk flows.
Research hints
💡 Compare supplier development (investing in suppliers) with supplier selection (threatening exit) in models of buyer-driven decarbonisation; when is the exit threat credible for a buyer this concentrated?
💡 Use a double-agency / multi-tier lens: processors sit between Nestlé and farms with their own incentives.
💡 Possible leakage: does milk from non-compliant farms simply move to other buyers, leaving sector emissions unchanged?
Data leadsCDP supply chain responses (Nestlé and dairy co-ops), SBTi target dashboard, Nestlé and co-op sustainability reports, USDA NASS dairy statistics; FactSet Revere for supplier links.
Keywordssupplier emissions · scope 3 disclosure · supplier switching · multi-tier traceability · sustainability standards
Related research
Eliciting Supplier Cooperation for Value Chain Decarbonization: A Field Experiment with Smallholder Farmers in India (Management Science, 2024)
A field experiment on getting farm suppliers to cooperate on value-chain decarbonisation, directly relevant to how Nestlé might induce farm-level change.
Sustainability in multi‐tier supply chains: Understanding the double agency role of the first‐tier supplier (Journal of Operations Management, 2015)
Examines when first-tier suppliers act as agents enforcing lead-firm sustainability requirements upstream, the processor role in Nestlé's dairy chain.
04 · Critical Minerals & Batteries

POSCO signs three-year lithium supply deal with CATL for 2026–2028

The Korea Herald · Nov 18 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov. 18, 2026, POSCO Group said it had finalised a three-year deal to supply lithium to CATL, which holds about 40% of the global EV battery market, covering 2026–2028. Deliveries have reportedly begun. Volume, price, the lithium's origin and its end market were not disclosed; Businesskorea reports the deal may involve recycled lithium as well as Argentine brine and Australian ore.

BackgroundPOSCO runs a brine lithium plant in Argentina and holds an equity stake and supply rights in Australia. Last month it finished the upstream part of a second Argentine brine plant, bringing annual capacity to 48,000 tons. It targets 173,000 tons of annual capacity by 2033 and more than 1.8 trillion won ($1.35 billion) in lithium operating profit by 2035. Businesskorea says the deal follows a supply deal with SK On and cooperation with China's Ronbay earlier this year.
ProblemPOSCO, a non-Chinese miner and processor, is selling to the dominant Chinese battery maker. This helps it fill new capacity, but undisclosed terms leave unclear how price risk and volume commitments are shared. The deal also raises questions about how such cross-border flows interact with export-control and sourcing-rule volatility. Businesskorea's text cites a conflicting 2027–2029 period; the Korea Herald, SMM and PKX confirm 2026–2028.
Where & whyThis is the upstream lithium link between resource developers and cell makers. Lumpy, long-lead capacity additions must be matched with offtake from a highly concentrated buyer base, with CATL alone holding about 40% of the EV battery market. Geopolitics adds another layer: material sourced from Argentina and Australia may carry different regulatory value depending on whether it ends up in Chinese or non-Chinese battery supply chains.
Stakeholders
POSCO Group — Securing offtake for expanding lithium capacity toward its 2033–2035 targets.
CATL — Diversified lithium supply, possibly including recycled lithium, from non-Chinese sources.
Korean battery makers (e.g., SK On) — Compete with CATL for POSCO's lithium output.
US and allied policymakers — Want allied mineral capacity to reduce reliance on China; sales to CATL complicate that aim.
Research hints
💡 Contract design for upstream capacity: why choose a fixed three-year term with a concentrated buyer over spot sales, and how might price-risk sharing be structured?
💡 Compare POSCO's customer portfolio (SK On, Ronbay, CATL) as a supplier-side diversification decision.
💡 Watch how 'foreign entity of concern' type rules change the value of non-Chinese lithium depending on the buyer.

05 · Energy & AI Data Centers

US data centers cut transmission charges by avoiding a few peak hours, shifting costs onto other ratepayers

Reuters · Nov 17 · Read · ☆ Star

Reuters (Nov. 17, 2026) reports that data centers and other large users are saving millions by sharply cutting grid use during a few annual peak hours ('peak shaving'), which is legal under market rules and state tariffs. Per NRG Energy, a large Texas data center can avoid about $33.4 million in transmission costs by staying off the grid for one hour a year, either by curtailing use or running backup generators. PJM's independent market monitor says data center growth has already added $29.4 billion to PJM system costs, which are eventually passed to customers.

BackgroundGrid costs are partly allocated by each customer's demand at system peak hours. Grid operators do not publish summer peak hours in advance, so data centers hire forecasters to predict them. Large loads with backup generation can respond in ways households cannot. Dominion Energy has created a new rate class made up mostly of data centers. Reuters snippets also mention rules requiring new data centers to sign 14-year contracts with minimum payments, but who set them is unconfirmed.
ProblemPeak shaving may lower system peaks when supply is tightest, as the Data Center Coalition argues. It also lets large users avoid allocated transmission costs that then shift to households. Will Baker of Everyday Electric says households cannot cut their charges the same way. One Spring, Texas customer's summer bill reached nearly $600. NRG says rule changes should limit cost-shifting to households. The total cost shifted is unknown. (Note: these details come from syndicated snippets; the full Reuters text was not available.)
Where & whyThe problem sits in tariff design and cost allocation: transmission costs are tied to coincident-peak demand in a handful of hours. The setup rewards forecasting the peak and briefly avoiding the grid. Large users have better forecasting capability and backup generation than households, which creates asymmetric ability to respond. Whether brief avoidance reduces the long-run cost drivers it is billed on is unclear.
Stakeholders
Data center operators (Data Center Coalition) — Large bill savings from peak shaving; argue it lowers system stress.
Residential customers — May absorb costs shifted from large users and cannot shave peaks themselves.
Utilities and retailers (Dominion, NRG) — Designing rate classes and contracts that recover grid costs without driving away large loads.
Grid operators and market monitors (ERCOT, PJM) — Want reliability and efficient cost allocation as large-load growth raises system costs.
State regulators — Deciding whether to keep, reprice or restrict coincident-peak cost allocation.
Research hints
💡 Mechanism-design lens: does coincident-peak cost allocation reward true flexibility or just peak-hour avoidance? Compare it with alternatives such as demand charges or minimum-take contracts.
💡 Strategic forecasting as a game: when many large loads predict the same peak hour, the peak can shift, the 'chasing the peak' problem.
💡 Backup generators used for peak avoidance have an emissions side; compare bill savings with local air-quality costs.
Data leadsERCOT 4CP (four coincident peak) hours and load data, EIA-930 hourly demand, PJM Data Miner load and capacity results, state PUC and FERC tariff dockets (e.g., Dominion's data center rate class).
Keywordslarge-load tariffs · demand response · cost pass-through · interruptible load · capacity markets
Thread to pull

Two of today's energy items describe the same kind of uncertain large load from opposite sides. Orennia models how lost data center demand would hit utility and generator economics, while Reuters shows those same loads strategically shaving the few peak hours that set their cost share. Utilities must commit to capacity for loads that may never connect and, once connected, can partly dodge the costs used to recover that capacity. That pairing makes a rich setting for linking capacity planning to tariff design, including commitment devices like the reported 14-year minimum-payment contracts.

Biodiversity Risk in the Corporate Bond Market

After the October 2021 COP15 biodiversity conference, US corporate bond investors began pricing biodiversity risk. A one-standard-deviation larger biodiversity footprint is associated with 7% higher yield spreads after COP15, a relationship that was insignificant before.
