01 · Critical Minerals & Batteries

DRC to halt all cobalt exports for four months from November 30, 2026, and caps 2027 exports at 65,250 tonnes

Benchmark Mineral Intelligence · Nov 23 · Read · ☆ Star

On November 23, the DRC's strategic minerals regulator ARECOMS announced a four-month ban on all cobalt exports starting 30 November 2026. The ban covers industrial, semi-industrial, small-scale and artisanal output. Benchmark estimates that the 2027 export cap of 65,250 t removes 29,000 t from the market. That pushes the projected deficit from -48kt to -54kt in 2026 and from -22kt to -40kt in 2027, or to -127kt in 2027 if only material that can actually leave the DRC is counted. Refined cobalt on China's electronic trading platform jumped from about 260,000 yuan/mt to above 300,000 yuan/mt in early trading.

BackgroundARECOMS previously banned exports in February 2025 and in October 2025 let specific miners resume under quotas; the 2026 annual quota was 96,600 t. Cobalt prices had fallen from about $56,300/t on July 1, 2026 to $33,525/t on November 20, a 30.98% year-on-year decline. ARECOMS cites slowing global consumption, rising inventories and speculative trading as its reasons for the ban.
ProblemA dominant producer is using export bans and quotas to manage price. This leaves battery and cathode makers unsure how much material will be available and when. Ending the ban does not automatically restart exports: a separate decision must set volumes, quota allocations and conditions. Existing 2026–2027 export rights do not guarantee the same volumes when shipments resume. Shanghai Metals Market notes it is still unverified whether downstream buyers will pay higher prices.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the export border between DRC mines and offshore refiners. It stems from supplier concentration combined with discretionary, opaque quota policy. Measured market balances can also mislead: Benchmark's balance includes CMOC material that cannot be exported, so headline inventory differs from what refiners can actually access. Buyers face policy uncertainty about the restart rules, not only a fixed supply cut.
Stakeholders
ARECOMS / DRC government — Wants to lift prices and capture more value from cobalt without permanently losing market share
DRC miners incl. CMOC and artisanal operators — Lose export volume and face uncertain quota allocations when shipments resume
Chinese refiners and cathode makers — Exposed to feedstock shortages and price spikes; must choose between drawing down inventory and finding alternative sources
Battery and EV manufacturers — Face cost pass-through and an incentive to shift toward lower-cobalt or cobalt-free chemistries
Research hints
💡 Repeated bans (Feb 2025, Nov 2026) separated by a quota period offer a sequence of policy shocks: compare how downstream inventory and price responses differ across them.
💡 Lens: strategic stockpiling and procurement under policy-driven supply uncertainty, where the timing and size of reopening is itself random.
💡 Puzzle: the ban comes after a 31% price drop, so a cartel-like supply-management model may fit better than a disruption model.
Data leadsUN Comtrade and USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries for DRC cobalt trade flows and production; Benchmark/BNEF balance data (commercial); cathode and battery makers' annual reports for inventory responses.
Keywordsexport controls · raw material security · supplier concentration · strategic stockpiling · battery chemistry shift
Related research

Uses cobalt mining as a motivating case for supply chain traceability systems, which matter for verifying origin and quota compliance across artisanal and industrial DRC supply.
02 · ESG & Disclosure

US Labor Department proposes removing ESG tiebreaker and participant-preference provisions from ERISA investment rule

ESG Dive · Nov 23 · Read · ☆ Star

On Monday, November 23, 2026, the Department of Labor proposed the rule "Loyalty and the Exclusive Purpose Rule in Selecting Plan Investments and Exercising Shareholder Rights." It would remove ESG terminology from the ERISA investment rule, reversing the 2022 Biden-era rule. The proposal drops the 'tiebreaker' test and the 'participant preference provision', and puts 'a heightened focus' on proxy voting and shareholder engagement by ERISA-covered managers. A 60-day comment period is reported.

BackgroundUnder the tiebreaker test, a plan sponsor could pick an ESG fund for its social benefits if it was 'tied' with another fund on financial factors. Republican-led states challenged the Biden-era rule through 2024. According to the Plan Sponsor Council of America, about 5% of 401(k) plans offered an ESG fund in 2025, and on average less than 1% of plan assets sat in ESG funds.
ProblemThe proposal narrows when fiduciaries can weigh non-financial benefits and increases scrutiny of how managers vote proxies. The DOL says it would not create 'a categorical prohibition on considering any type of information.' It is unclear whether the change will mainly alter fund menus, which hold little money, or alter stewardship pressure on portfolio companies through proxy voting.
Where & whyThe friction sits in the delegation chain from plan participants to sponsors, then asset managers, then portfolio firms. Fiduciary litigation risk and shifting federal rules change managers' incentives to support environmental and social proposals, independent of how much money is in ESG funds. Policy has flipped repeatedly across administrations, so the rules themselves are uncertain.
Stakeholders
Department of Labor (Trump administration) — Wants to refocus ERISA fiduciary duty on financial factors only
401(k) plan sponsors — Seek legal safe harbor and lower litigation risk when choosing fund menus
ESG fund managers and proxy advisors — May lose flows and face scrutiny of their voting on environmental and social proposals
Plan participants — Lose a formal channel for expressing preferences for ESG options
Portfolio firms — May face less investor pressure on emissions or supply chain practices
Research hints
💡 Repeated rule flips (2020, 2022, 2026) give event dates for checking whether ERISA-heavy shareholders change their votes on environmental and social proposals.
💡 The mechanism likely runs through proxy voting rather than fund flows, since ESG funds hold under 1% of assets; think about where ESG capital pressure actually binds on operations.
💡 Lens: how investor pressure affects firms' cost of capital and their emissions or supplier decisions.
Data leadsFederal Register docket and comment letters; SEC EDGAR Form N-PX proxy voting records; DOL Form 5500 filings for plan menus.
Keywordsfund labeling · regulatory uncertainty · proxy voting · erisa fiduciary duty
Related research
Climate Impact Investing (Management Science, 2022)
Models how green investors' wealth share raises carbon-intensive firms' cost of capital and lowers emissions, the channel a weaker ESG mandate could dampen.
Tailor-to-Target: Configuring Collaborative Shareholder Engagements on Climate Change (Management Science, 2023)
Studies collaborative climate engagements by investor coalitions, relevant to the proposal's heightened focus on engagement and proxy voting.
03 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Ohio Power Siting Board approves 800 MW off-grid gas plant for Ashville data center after 81% of local voters opposed the deal

Canary Media · Nov 23 · Read · ☆ Star

The Ohio Power Siting Board approved an 800 MW gas plant to serve an EdgeConneX data center complex in Ashville, central Ohio. The plant will not be connected to the wider grid and will serve only the data centers. The approval came after 81% of village voters opposed the local agreement with EdgeConneX in this month's election. The board also approved the Fairfield Energy Center to power four data centers in Millersport.

BackgroundIn April, Ashville's Village Council backed the project after EdgeConneX agreed to pay up to $102 million for municipal, school and community work. Statewide, all 18 local data center moratoriums and exclusions on the ballot passed overwhelmingly. The board found that the project met the statutory public-interest requirement. EdgeConneX says in regulatory filings that the vote had no 'any effect' on the plant or the data center.
ProblemLocal democratic rejection is colliding with state-level siting authority. This raises the question of who decides where AI-driven generation is built. Critics say the board treats gas more leniently than renewables, having denied renewable projects that faced less opposition. Choosing dedicated off-grid generation also sidesteps grid interconnection and spreads costs and emissions differently than buying grid power.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the siting and permitting stage, where state and local jurisdiction overlap. Developers who face interconnection delays and fast-growing load choose behind-the-meter gas. Local residents bear the externalities but have limited formal veto power. The board's treatment of different technologies may tilt that choice.
Stakeholders
EdgeConneX and data center tenants — Want fast, dedicated power and certainty that their project can proceed
Ashville and Millersport residents — Bear local impacts; their 81% vote may carry no legal effect
Ohio Power Siting Board — Must balance its public-interest finding against perceived bias toward gas over renewables
Village government and schools — Stand to receive up to $102 million in developer payments
Renewable developers — May face stricter siting treatment than gas projects
Research hints
💡 Compare Siting Board outcomes for gas versus solar and wind cases with similar levels of opposition.
💡 Model family: a data center's choice between on-site generation and grid supply under interconnection delay and siting risk.
💡 The 18 ballot measures offer a cross-section of local siting shocks for testing where developers relocate.
Data leadsOhio Power Siting Board dockets (state PUC records); EIA-860 for planned gas units; county ballot results for the 18 moratorium measures.
Keywordsfacility siting · siting moratorium · gas turbines · data center electricity demand · federal-state jurisdiction
Related research
Meeting Corporate Renewable Power Targets (Management Science, 2018)
Studies how firms meet corporate renewable power targets, the procurement alternative to building dedicated gas generation.
Decarbonizing OCP (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2023)
Optimizes an industrial firm's on-site electricity investment, a template for analyzing behind-the-meter supply decisions by large loads.
04 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Dell, Lenovo, Supermicro and HPE reportedly raise AI server prices 25–45% after contracts are signed, citing memory shortage

DatacenterDynamics · Nov 23 · Read · ☆ Star

Citing unnamed sources, the Financial Times reports that Dell, Lenovo, Supermicro and HPE are demanding price increases of 25% to 45% when equipment is ready to ship, months after contracts were signed. GPU server prices are up at least 20–30%, while CPU nodes have tripled or more. Buyers hit include non-hyperscalers such as financial trading firms. Court filings allege that Dell told XTX that other customers had explored alternative suppliers but returned and accepted the higher prices.

BackgroundThe FT reports memory costs up more than 400% since early 2024. TrendForce says server DRAM contract prices rose 64% in the second half of 2025 alone and could reach 270% by year-end. It also expects high-bandwidth memory to rise 70–140% in 2027. SK Hynix has pushed its expected date for relief from 2028 to 2030.
ProblemSigned contracts are failing to fix prices when a component bottleneck hits. OEMs pass input costs to buyers at delivery, and buyers find switching ineffective. It is contested whether this reflects genuine cost pass-through or holdup by concentrated suppliers. The breakdown by vendor and amount is not public, and the OEMs have not confirmed the reports.
Where & whyThe friction sits in the OEM-to-buyer contracting link, downstream of a memory capacity shortfall. Incomplete contracts, long lead times and a concentrated supply base give OEMs leverage at delivery. Smaller buyers without hyperscale bargaining power may be hit hardest.
Stakeholders
Server OEMs (Dell, Lenovo, Supermicro, HPE) — Protect margins against memory cost spikes, at the risk of litigation and lost customer trust
Non-hyperscale buyers (e.g., trading firm XTX) — Face budget overruns and weak outside options
Memory makers (e.g., SK Hynix) — Capture pricing power while their capacity remains constrained until about 2030
Hyperscalers — May secure priority allocation and better terms, widening the gap with smaller buyers
Research hints
💡 Lens: holdup and renegotiation in incomplete contracts when input prices spike after signing.
💡 Compare pass-through rates across buyer types (hyperscaler versus enterprise) and across server types (GPU versus CPU nodes).
💡 Puzzle: CPU node prices tripled while GPU servers rose 20–30%. What does memory's share of the bill of materials predict?
Data leadsSEC EDGAR 10-K/10-Q filings and earnings call transcripts for OEM gross margins and pricing commentary; TrendForce DRAM contract price series; court docket for the XTX–Dell case.
Keywordscost pass-through · long-term supply contracts · supplier switching · supplier concentration · capacity investment under uncertainty
Related research
Supply Chain Relationships and Contracts: The Impact of Repeated Interaction on Capacity Investment and Procurement (Management Science, 2007)
Analyzes how repeated interaction sustains capacity and procurement terms when court-enforceable contracts are infeasible, directly relevant to post-signing price renegotiation.
Supplier Competition with Option Contracts for Discrete Blocks of Capacity (Operations Research, 2017)
Models competing suppliers' reservation and execution prices for capacity blocks, a lens on how buyers could hedge component shortages contractually.
05 · Supply Chain Resilience

Far East–US East Coast spot container rates reach $10,901 per FEU, up 297.8% year over year, as capacity grows

FreightWaves · Nov 23 · Read · ☆ Star

For the week ending November 19, Xeneta data show Far East–US East Coast rates fell 1.9% to $10,901 per FEU, still 297.8% higher than a year earlier. West Coast rates fell 1.7% to $8,203, and North Europe rates fell 2.3% to $3,438. On a four-week rolling basis, capacity has risen 10.9% to the West Coast and 11.4% to the East Coast since October 19.

BackgroundEast Coast rates peaked at $11,259 per FEU on September 17. Xeneta's Peter Sand says offered capacity is increasing on all fronthauls out of the Far East and spot rates continue to soften, with one exception. One trade outlet attributes the surge to a 'Hormuz crisis', with rates over 330% above their February 28 levels, but FreightWaves does not confirm this cause.
ProblemShippers face spot rates roughly four times last year's levels. Capacity is returning, so they must decide whether to lock in contract rates, ride a falling spot market, or reroute to West Coast ports and move goods inland. The unresolved question is how fast added capacity will bring rates down and how persistent the East Coast premium is.
Where & whyThe friction sits in the transpacific and all-water East Coast ocean lanes. Capacity takes time to redeploy after a disruption, so supply lags demand and rates overshoot. The East–West Coast spread reflects differences in route exposure and inland alternatives.
Stakeholders
US importers and retailers — Higher landed costs; must choose between contract and spot procurement and set inventory buffers
Ocean carriers — Earn elevated margins but risk a rate collapse as capacity is added
East and West Coast ports and intermodal rail — Gain or lose volume depending on rerouting decisions
Freight forwarders — Face margin and risk exposure from the gap between contract and spot rates
Research hints
💡 Track the East–West Coast rate spread as a measure of route-specific disruption, and check whether import volumes shift toward West Coast ports.
💡 Model family: shipper choice of a carrier portfolio and mix of contract and spot freight while rates fall from a peak.
💡 Verify the claimed Hormuz-crisis cause before using February 28 as an event date.
Data leadsXeneta/Sea-Intelligence rate and reliability data (commercial) or the public Drewry World Container Index; NOAA AIS vessel tracks and Census USA Trade Online for port-level import shifts.
Keywordsfreight rates · chokepoint disruption · capacity utilization · port congestion · long-term supply contracts
Related research
Carrier Portfolio Management for Shipping Seasonal Products (Operations Research, 2017)
Studies a shipper's choice among vessels with different schedules and freight rates, a direct lens on carrier and route selection when lane rates diverge.
The signaling effect of supplier's customer network instability on service price: Insights from the container shipping charter market (Journal of Operations Management, 2023)
Uses container charter market data to show how a supplier's network instability signals reliability and affects service prices.
Thread to pull

The cobalt ban and the AI server price hikes are both cases where a concentrated upstream supplier changes terms after buyers have committed: the DRC through discretionary quota restarts, OEMs through repricing at delivery. Comparing how contracts, inventory and switching options protect (or fail to protect) buyers in each case could clarify when resilience comes from contract design versus physical buffers. The Ohio gas plant adds another layer: data center operators facing rising hardware and power costs at once may increasingly vertically integrate into their own generation.

New in the journals

Inventory Productivity and Carbon Emissions in Retailing
M&SOM

Using CDP emissions data matched to retailer financials, the paper finds that most emission reductions associated with higher inventory productivity come from scale. Holding scale fixed, an external shock to inventory productivity raises emission intensity, especially in Scope 3 purchased goods and services.

Today's supply shocks (cobalt, memory, freight) are pushing firms to re-tune inventory, and this paper warns that leaner inventory can carry hidden Scope 3 costs.

How Not to Overpackage: AI for Sustainability in HelloFresh’s Service Supply Chain
M&SOM

Working with HelloFresh, the authors define a Contextual Packaging problem: how much and what type of insulation to put in each meal-kit box. They develop an online-learning method that uses the problem's structure to cut packaging waste and emissions while preserving product quality.

A clear example of operational analytics delivering measurable sustainability gains, useful as a template for data-driven waste reduction decisions in circular economy work.
