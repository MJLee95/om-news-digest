US remote-access AI chip rule and supply-chain fallout

Last updated Nov 20, 2026 · first opened Nov 20, 2026

BIS's 19 Nov 2026 interim final rule extends US advanced-chip controls to overseas data centers that rent significant compute to China-linked firms. It also adds license requirements for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, and tightens rules for foundries and packagers. Early industry reports say the disruption reaches well beyond AI chips and China, though this is not yet verified.

Background

The US first imposed sweeping controls on advanced computing chips to China in Oct 2022 and tightened them further in later rounds. In Jan 2025 the Biden administration issued two rules. The AI Diffusion Rule set up tiered worldwide licensing, partly to stop China renting compute abroad. A Foundry Due Diligence rule created a presumption that certain custom chips are advanced data-center chips. In May 2025 the Trump administration said it would not enforce the Diffusion Rule and promised a 'stronger but simpler' replacement. From mid-2025 the Justice Department brought criminal cases alleging GPUs were routed to China via Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand. On 16 Nov 2026 (a Sunday) BIS released the interim final rule 'Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors' (RIN 0694-AK50). It was issued without prior notice and comment under ECRA §1762(a) and was credited as written by and at the direction of Treasury and the National Security Council. It was published in the Federal Register (2026-23529) and took effect on 19 Nov 2026, reportedly days before a Trump–Xi meeting.

Why it is happening

Earlier controls covered physical shipments of chips. Chinese firms could still rent advanced compute in third-country data centers, including facilities built largely for them, and chips were allegedly smuggled through Southeast Asia. BIS says the aim is to undermine China-linked entities' ability to access significant volumes of advanced chips remotely, for both training and running AI models. The rule also delivers the long-promised replacement for the rescinded Diffusion Rule, and its timing appears tied to US–China negotiations.

Where things stand

The rule has been in force since 19 Nov 2026, and it formally repeals the AI Diffusion Rule. Key features:

  • Data-center control: a license is needed to ship advanced chips to any data center supplying more than 12 million TPP (roughly 200 top-end GPUs, per Gibson Dunn) to China/D:5-headquartered firms. Linked sites are counted together, and access provided through a middleman is covered.
  • Due diligence: exporters are presumed to 'know' the risk if a data center withholds its know-your-customer information.
  • Exception: commercial recommender systems are exempt; generative systems are not.
  • Five-country requirement: licenses are now needed for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, with a reporting carve-out for US-headquartered customers.
  • Transition: a 30-day savings clause covers shipments already en route.

Industry executives told Nikkei the impact is far wider than expected, reaching consumer and mobile chips and chip designers outside China. The reported TSMC and Samsung shipment halts are unverified. Open questions include how the threshold and linked-site counting work, how far the 'to or for' language reaches, liability for past deals, and how BIS will treat existing deployments made in good faith. The reactions of China and the five countries have not been verified.

What to watch

  • ~17 Feb 2027: 90-day deadline for existing data centers serving China-linked firms to obtain licenses (calculated from 19 Nov)
  • 18–19 Jan 2027: public comment deadline on the interim final rule (the two sources differ on the exact date)
  • Outcome of the Trump–Xi meeting and any adjustment to the chip controls
