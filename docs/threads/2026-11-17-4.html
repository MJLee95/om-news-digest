US remote-access AI chip rule and supply-chain fallout

Last updated Nov 27, 2026 · first opened Nov 20, 2026

BIS's interim final rule, in force since 19 Nov 2026, extends US advanced-chip controls to overseas data centers that rent significant compute to China-linked firms. It also adds license requirements for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil. Nvidia has reportedly paused server shipments to the four Southeast Asian countries, and broader foundry disruption is reported but unverified.

Background

Key steps before the rule:

  • Oct 2022: the US first imposed controls on advanced computing chips to China.
  • Oct/Nov 2023: licenses became required for Country Groups D:1, D:4 and D:5. BIS now says sales 'on behalf of' Chinese firms, including a Chinese firm using more than 50% of a data center's compute, have been covered since then.
  • 15 Jan 2025: the Biden AI Diffusion Rule (90 FR 4544) set up tiered worldwide licensing.
  • May 2025: BIS said it would withdraw the Diffusion Rule. The new rule says it 'has never been enforced.'
  • 2024–June 2025: per a Commerce–Justice statement reported only secondhand, Commerce investigated Singapore-based Megaspeed, and Justice took over the criminal case after a June 2025 on-site check.
  • 14 Jul 2026: a rule gave the UAE 'Enhanced Favorable Treatment'; the new rule refers back to it.
  • 16 Nov 2026: BIS released the interim final rule 'Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors' (RIN 0694-AK50). It was directed by Treasury and the National Security Council.
  • 19 Nov 2026: the rule was published (91 FR 74970, FR Doc 2026-23529) and took effect, without prior notice and comment.

Why it is happening

Earlier controls governed where chips were shipped. Chinese firms could still rent advanced compute in third-country data centers, the so-called 'remote access gap.' Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia had become popular with Chinese AI developers working around US limits, and chips were allegedly diverted through the region (e.g., the Megaspeed case, still alleged only). The official rationale is military and intelligence risk from advanced AI models: weapons development, cyber-espionage and disinformation. The rule also delivers the promised replacement for the Diffusion Rule. DLA Piper links it to growing congressional scrutiny.

Where things stand

The rule has been in force since 19 Nov 2026 and formally repeals the Diffusion Rule.

  • Data-center control (§744.24): a license is needed when chips go to any data center giving China/Macau/D:5-headquartered firms access to more than 12M aggregate TPP (roughly 200 top-end GPUs, per Gibson Dunn). Linked sites count together, including digitally linked ones, and access through a middleman is covered.
  • Exemption: recommender systems are exempt.
  • Five-country requirement: licenses are now needed for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, with a reporting exemption for US-headquartered customers (§743.10).
  • Nvidia pause: reported by the FT on 26 Nov and carried by Reuters. It covers the four Southeast Asian countries but not Brazil. Its duration is unknown, and one report mentions a process to eventually release shipments.
  • Entity List: Commerce reportedly added seven firms in Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand, including Megaspeed, on 23 Nov. The Federal Register notice has not been found.

Still open:

  • license timelines and outcomes;
  • scope of the 'to or for' and linked-site provisions;
  • liability for past deals;
  • whether the foundry halts (TSMC/Samsung) are real and how large they are;
  • possible changes after comments, since this is an interim rule.

Earlier versions of this brief

Brief as of Nov 20, 2026: US remote-access AI chip rule and supply-chain fallout

BIS's 19 Nov 2026 interim final rule extends US advanced-chip controls to overseas data centers that rent significant compute to China-linked firms. It also adds license requirements for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, and tightens rules for foundries and packagers. Early industry reports say the disruption reaches well beyond AI chips and China, though this is not yet verified.

Status then: The rule has been in force since 19 Nov 2026, and it formally repeals the AI Diffusion Rule. Key features: - **Data-center control:** a license is needed to ship advanced chips to any data center supplying more than 12 million TPP (roughly 200 top-end GPUs, per Gibson Dunn) to China/D:5-headquartered firms. Linked sites are counted together, and access provided through a middleman is covered. - **Due diligence:** exporters are presumed to 'know' the risk if a data center withholds its know-your-customer information. - **Exception:** commercial recommender systems are exempt; generative systems are not. - **Five-country requirement:** licenses are now needed for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, with a reporting carve-out for US-headquartered customers. - **Transition:** a 30-day savings clause covers shipments already en route. Industry executives told Nikkei the impact is far wider than expected, reaching consumer and mobile chips and chip designers outside China. The reported TSMC and Samsung shipment halts are unverified. Open questions include how the threshold and linked-site counting work, how far the 'to or for' language reaches, liability for past deals, and how BIS will treat existing deployments made in good faith. The reactions of China and the five countries have not been verified.
