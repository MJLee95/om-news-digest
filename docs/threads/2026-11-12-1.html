Georgia Power's data-center gas buildout meets a Democratic PSC

Last updated Nov 15, 2026 · first opened Nov 15, 2026

Georgia Power is building about 10 GW of mostly data-center-driven capacity, approved in December 2025, and is procuring another 2–6 GW for 2032–2033. The November 2026 election gives Democrats a 3–2 majority on the Public Service Commission from January 2027, and the new commissioners say they will challenge the gas expansion and the bill increases tied to it.

Background

In January 2025 the PSC adopted rules requiring large new customers such as data centers to pay the costs of serving them. On July 1, 2025 it voted 5–0 to freeze base rates through at least 2028. Storm costs, mostly from Hurricane Helene, were excluded, and critics called it a freeze 'in name only.' In July 2025 the PSC approved the 2025 integrated resource plan, which allowed 6,000–8,500 MW of new supply 'based on proven need'; the exact vote date is unconfirmed. In November 2025 Democrat Peter Hubbard won a seat, and Democrat Alicia Johnson also joined the commission. On December 19, 2025, before the two Democrats were seated, the all-Republican PSC voted 5–0 to approve a negotiated deal. It covers about 9,885 MW at about $16.3B: 3.6 GW of new gas, 3 GW of batteries, 350 MW of solar paired with storage and about 2.8 GW of purchased power. About 80% is for data centers, and the deal skipped competitive bidding. PSC staff had reportedly first recommended approving only about a third of that right away; staff's final position is unclear. In February 2026 the PSC voted 3–2 to reject environmental groups' request for reconsideration, with Hubbard and Alicia Johnson dissenting. In April 2026 Georgia Power requested another 2–6 GW of dispatchable capacity for 2032–2033.

Why it is happening

Georgia Power forecasts a surge in data-center load and expects regional demand to roughly triple by the mid-2030s. Independent estimates are far lower: researchers cited by Bisnow expect only 2.4–6.7 GW of data-center growth. The PSC has approved six rate increases in recent years, voters are angry about bills, and another increase looms when the freeze ends in 2028. The core dispute is about who bears the risk if data-center demand fails to materialize. Hubbard put it this way: 'the roommate moves out, but the mortgage doesn't go away.' A separate fight over capacity accreditation (ELCC) shapes whether gas or batteries win new procurements.

Where things stand

The December 2025 approval of about 10 GW remains in force, though Canary reports Georgia Power has made 'little progress' carrying it out. The 2032–33 procurement for 2–6 GW is under way. SELC attorney Bob Sherrier says it credits batteries with only 20% ELCC, against about 80% for gas, while the utility's earlier figures imply about 75% for batteries. Earlier plans rated batteries at 95% for the first 3,000 MW, then 75%, then 50%. This account has not been checked against the procurement documents. Democrats take control in January 2027. Reports put the length of prior Republican control at 30–31 years, back to roughly 1995. Whether and how the new majority will reopen the December 2025 order or change the procurement is still open. A base-rate case is expected around 2028, when the freeze ends.

What to watch

  • January 2027: Shelia Edwards is seated and the Democratic majority takes control. Watch for any motion to open proceedings on the December 2025 plan's costs and climate impacts
  • 2027: possible PSC action on the ELCC values used in the 2032–33 procurement
  • Mid-2027 (per Inside Climate News): selected 2032–33 projects go to the PSC for approval
  • 2028: the base-rate freeze ends and a rate case is likely
