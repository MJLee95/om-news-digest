Daily Clippings · Weekly review

Week ending November 15

This week was dominated by AI data-center load, seen from several angles: Norway froze grid reservations, PJM's market monitor put a $29.4B price on data-center capacity, Georgia's utility regulator flipped party control, and an Irish court challenge kept Dublin's connection rules in doubt. In each case a speculative pipeline far exceeds signed load, and the fight is over who bears the risk. Alongside that, trade and minerals policy moved on short, uncertain horizons (China's two-month extension, a Mexico tariff threat, Canada's default notice to Stellantis, India's black-mass export ban). ESG disclosure fragmented further: the Ninth Circuit split Scope 3 from Scope 1–2, while SBTi, CSRD assurance and UK deforestation rules all opened consultations.

Research ideas

Idea 1

Screening speculative large loads: moratoria vs. collateral vs. accreditation

QuestionDo large-load screening mechanisms (minimum bills, collateral and exit fees in US large-load tariffs; Norway's reservation freeze; Ireland's self-supply rule) reduce the gap between requested and realized data-center load? Do they shift siting across jurisdictions, and at what cost to time-to-power?
Why nowMany sources of variation arrived at once. Norway froze reservations on a dated schedule. PPL publishes a quarterly 11-quarter series of advanced vs signed vs under-construction GW. Georgia's regulator changed control. PJM's monitor quantified the socialized cost. US utilities are adopting large-load tariffs at different dates.
DataPPL 8-K/10-Q pipeline disclosures (quarterly); Georgia PSC and other state PUC large-load tariff dockets (adoption dates and terms); PJM load forecasts and capacity auction results; LBNL interconnection queue data; EIA-860 planned generators; Statnett reservation statistics plus ENTSO-E and Nord Pool data for Nordic siting spillovers; Interconnected Capital moratorium tracker.
IdentificationStaggered difference-in-differences on utility-quarter pipeline conversion (signed/requested, withdrawals), with treatment defined as adoption of a large-load tariff with financial commitments. A secondary design treats the Norway freeze as a siting shock and tests for diversion of announcements to Sweden, Finland and Denmark. Main threat: adoption is endogenous, because utilities with the most inflated queues adopt first. Common AI-demand and chip-supply shocks also move all pipelines together. Event-time pre-trends and utility-specific trends are needed, and developers filing duplicate requests across utilities complicates the counts.
LiteratureExtends Capacity Investment with Demand Learning to a setting where demand signals are strategic and cheap to send. Peak-Load Energy Management by Direct Load Control Contracts offers a contract-design analogue for shifting risk onto large loads. How Sustainable Is Big Data? motivates the system-level stakes.

Idea 2

Compliance-driven or strategic? Supplier emissions data after the Scope 3 mandate weakens

QuestionAfter the Ninth Circuit signaled that California's Scope 3 mandate is likely unconstitutional, do covered buyers reduce supplier emissions data requests and supplier engagement, relative to firms that remain bound by other regimes (EU CSRD, SBTi targets)?
Why nowThe ruling split Scope 1–2 (still mandated, first filings Nov 10) from Scope 3 (likely enjoined) within the same firms. At the same time, the EU opened a CSRD assurance consultation and SBTi is finalizing Standard 2.0. That gives a clean contrast between firms that keep an external Scope 3 obligation and firms that lose theirs.
DataCDP Supply Chain program data (buyer requests, supplier response rates, supplier-reported emissions); SEC EDGAR 10-K climate language; CARB SB 253 filing records; SBTi target dashboard; CSRD sustainability statements and assurance reports for EU-exposed US firms; FactSet Revere or Compustat segments for buyer–supplier links.
IdentificationTriple difference: California exposure × absence of a CSRD/SBTi Scope 3 obligation × post-ruling, with supplier-level outcomes (request receipt, disclosure, emissions trajectory). The within-firm Scope 1–2 vs Scope 3 contrast provides a placebo. Main threat: anticipation. The 2025 SB 261 stay and ongoing litigation may already have shifted behavior, so the ruling may be partly priced in. Investor and customer pressure that correlates with CSRD exposure could also confound the result.
LiteratureTests the signaling channel in Customer base environmental disclosure and supplier greenhouse gas emissions: A signaling theory perspective under mandate withdrawal. Relates to Are Firms Voluntarily Disclosing Emissions Greener? on voluntary vs mandated disclosure, and to Sustainable evaluation and verification in supply chains on how verification regimes shape data quality.

Idea 3

Short-horizon rolling suspensions and precautionary inventory in critical minerals

QuestionHow does the remaining length of a suspended export control (here, two months) affect downstream stockpiling and supplier qualification, compared with an indefinite suspension or an active control?
Why nowChina's controls now have a sequence of event windows: April 2025 imposition, October 2025 expansion, November 2025 one-year suspension, and the November 2026 two-month extension. The suspension horizon therefore varies over time while the underlying product scope stays fixed. Concurrent footprint moves (BMW adding Korean cell suppliers) and India's black-mass ban add parallel resource-nationalism shocks.
DataChina customs (GACC) monthly exports of rare-earth magnets, graphite anode and Li-ion cells by destination; UN Comtrade and USITC DataWeb partner imports; USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries; 10-K and earnings-call mentions of inventory buffers and supplier qualification; automaker cell-supplier announcements.
IdentificationEvent study on destination-product monthly flows across the four windows. The test is for import surges that scale with how short the remaining horizon is, using destinations more vs less exposed to US–China negotiations as the contrast. A newsvendor or real-options model with a stochastic snap-back date gives the predicted inventory response. Main threat: negotiation news, US tariff changes and downstream EV demand cycles move at the same time as the windows. Separating horizon length from changes in the perceived probability of reimposition is hard.
LiteratureBuilds on Balancing Resilience and Efficiency: A Literature Review on Overcoming Supply Chain Disruptions and Diversify or Concentrate? Supply Chain Responses to Policy Uncertainty by adding the horizon dimension of uncertainty. Offshoring technology innovation: A case study of rare-earth technology provides context on processing concentration.

Patterns this week

Speculative large-load queues and the fight over who bears stranded-capacity risk

Many grid operators reported requested or 'advanced' data-center load far above signed load: PPL has 35.3 GW advanced vs 11.4 GW signed, and Georgia Power 80.1 GW pipeline vs 12.8 GW contracted. Jurisdictions are responding with very different screening tools: a moratorium (Norway), self-supply mandates (Ireland, the PJM monitor's proposal), capacity accreditation choices (Georgia's ELCC), local NDA bans (Climate Mayors) and political turnover (Georgia PSC). Because these tools vary across places and time, they offer comparison cases for capacity-investment-under-uncertainty and mechanism-design work on how cheap queue positions should be priced.

Measurement and accounting choices quietly decide operational outcomes

Several stories turned on a technical parameter rather than on physical change. Examples: a 20% vs ~75% ELCC for batteries; a weight-based PET recycling rate that falls partly because bottles got lighter; a self-reported SB 54 baseline that understates tonnage; how emissions are allocated between a laptop's first and second life; SBTi deliverability regions; and book-and-claim freight certificates. For OM researchers these are tractable settings in which the metric design is itself the treatment, and the question is whether it changes technology choice, redesign or procurement.

Policy uncertainty with short or unclear horizons hits capacity and footprint decisions

Firms faced several kinds of uncertainty at once: a two-month rolling suspension of Chinese export controls, a tariff threat with no scope or legal basis, and a subsidy clawback after tariffs pulled Jeep production into Illinois. Footprint moves ranged from GM's dual US footprint to Deere offshoring a low-volume line to Brazil and a small Australian brand forward-positioning inventory in Texas after de minimis ended. Kearney's figures (capex tripled, capacity +1.5%) suggest that sourcing shifts quickly while domestic capacity does not. That gap between announcements and realized capacity is measurable.

States steering the processing stage of mineral and secondary-material chains

Governments intervened at the processing tier, where value and concentration sit. Examples: India's immediate black-mass export ban, Brazil's tiered critical-minerals list, Tianjin's zone-based steering of secondary-copper capacity, and China's control lever. France's large recycling plants stalled for lack of feedstock while smaller plants advanced, and US PET reclaimers lost capacity even as rPET imports hit a record share. Together these frame a recurring OM question: how does capacity get built when feedstock and offtake are both uncertain and policy-shaped?
