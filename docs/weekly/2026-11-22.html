Daily Clippings · Weekly review

Week ending November 22

Data-center power dominated the week. Regulators on three continents moved from debating AI load growth to rationing and pricing it: Korea's scoring rule, PJM's merged queue, utilities' FERC commitment filings, Spain's 80% renewables decree, and Oklahoma's court challenge. At the same time, evidence piled up that the demand those rules are built around is uncertain (Orennia's 191 GW at risk, community blocks, Meta's emissions jump). Outside energy, US remote-access chip controls spilled into mainstream chips, buyers tightened Scope 3 pressure on suppliers through money, exit threats and reporting platforms, and several dated EU and US policy shocks (plastic waste export ban, EUDR enforcement, UK-EU ETS link) created clean natural experiments.

Research ideas

Idea 1

Do long-term large-load commitments screen out speculative data-center demand?

QuestionDo minimum-term, take-or-pay and collateral requirements in large-load tariffs reduce speculative interconnection requests and later withdrawals? Do they shift requested data-center load toward jurisdictions with weaker commitments, and what happens to the residential rate path in adopting utilities?
Why nowTerms are being set right now with real cross-sectional variation: a 10-year PJM floor proposed against a 12-year state average, 15-year terms in CA/NY, up to 20 years in MISO, plus Oklahoma's HB 2992 and AEP Ohio's tariff. They are layered on top of a political demand shock (the midterms) and rising community denials. That separates screening effects from general demand softening better than any earlier period.
DataFERC eLibrary (ER27-531-000 and the compliance filings of other grid operators); state PUC large-load tariff dockets (Oklahoma PUD2026-000031, AEP Ohio, Alabama PSC, Dominion); ERCOT large-load interconnection reports; LBNL Queued Up; PJM load forecasts; Relae's blocked-project tracker; EIA-861 residential revenue by utility.
IdentificationStaggered difference-in-differences at the utility-territory level. Outcomes are new large-load request volume (MW), withdrawal rates and time to energization, comparing utilities before and after adopting a commitment tariff. Adjacent non-adopting territories in the same grid operator serve as controls, with term length as a continuous treatment. The main threat is reverse causality: utilities adopt strict terms precisely where speculative requests have surged, and adoption coincides with state political shocks such as new governors and moratoria. Address it with event-time pre-trends, a 'leakage' outcome measured in neighbouring territories, and placebo tests on generator queues.
LiteratureCapacity Investment with Demand Learning and Financing Capacity Investment Under Demand Uncertainty: An Optimal Contracting Approach supply the theory of committing capacity before demand resolves and of contract-based financing. Interplay between Competing and Coexisting Policy Regimens within Supply Chain Configurations speaks to overlapping federal and state regimes. That's Not Fair: Tariff Structures for Electricity Markets with Rooftop Solar frames the cross-subsidy question in tariff design.

Idea 2

When export controls follow the 'China+1' hubs: over-compliance and capacity reallocation

QuestionDid the November 2026 remote-access licensing rule reduce advanced-chip and server flows to the five covered countries relative to uncovered hubs? Did suppliers over-comply, cutting shipments of non-targeted mainstream chips beyond what the rule requires? And did announced data-center capacity relocate to uncovered hubs?
Why nowThe rule has a sharp effective date and a named set of treated countries, plus a 90-day cutoff for existing arrangements. Early reports of TSMC and Samsung halting ordinary smartphone-chip shipments make the over-compliance channel observable right away, rather than after years of adjustment.
DataTaiwan Ministry of Finance and Korea customs monthly exports by HS code (8542, 8471) and partner; UN Comtrade and Census USA Trade Online; the BIS rule text and licensing records; DatacenterDynamics and Structure Research capacity announcements for Southeast Asia, Japan, India and the Gulf; FactSet Revere foundry–customer links; 10-K risk factors on SEC EDGAR.
IdentificationA triple difference with three dimensions: covered versus uncovered destination countries, before versus after Nov. 19, and advanced (controlled) versus mainstream (non-targeted) product lines. A post-rule drop in mainstream lines to covered or Chinese destinations measures over-compliance. The main threat is confounding concurrent shocks: the threatened US tax on overseas AI data centers, front-loading ahead of an anticipated rule, and cyclical shifts in Chinese demand. Use pre-announcement windows and countries exposed to the tax threat but not to the rule.
LiteratureDiversify or Concentrate? Supply Chain Responses to Policy Uncertainty and Just-in-time for supply chains in turbulent times frame firms' sourcing and inventory responses. Global operations and supply-chain management under the political economy and Locking in overseas buyers amid geopolitical conflicts cover geopolitically driven buyer–supplier relationships.

Idea 3

Finance, threaten, or measure? Comparing buyer levers for supplier decarbonization

QuestionDo suppliers subject to different buyer interventions reduce their reported emissions intensity, and does non-compliant supply shift to less demanding buyers? The interventions to compare are blended finance (Inditex/D-TAFS), exit threats (Nestlé dairy), and a shared reporting platform (Tesco/OpenClimat). The leakage question is whether non-compliant supply simply moves elsewhere.
Why nowThree major buyers launched distinct levers in the same week, in different sectors, with identifiable supplier sets: Tesco's 400 key suppliers, IFC's 60–80 D-TAFS borrowers, and Nestlé's 200+ processors. Meanwhile Meta's possible target revision shows what happens when Scope 3 commitments meet capacity growth.
DataCDP supply chain responses; the SBTi target dashboard; Open Supply Hub factory lists linking suppliers to brands; IFC disclosure portal project documents for D-TAFS borrowers; FactSet Revere buyer–supplier links; Nestlé, Tesco and dairy co-op sustainability reports.
IdentificationSupplier-level difference-in-differences comparing suppliers of the intervening buyer with suppliers of peer buyers in the same sector and country. Outcomes are reported emissions intensity, SBTi target adoption and relationship persistence. The main threat is selection: buyers target suppliers already on a decarbonization path, and CDP reporting is itself endogenous to buyer pressure. Mitigate with pre-period matching on emissions trends, and with outcomes that don't depend on the supplier's own disclosure, such as SBTi status, or factory energy permits where available.
LiteratureEliciting Supplier Cooperation for Value Chain Decarbonization: A Field Experiment with Smallholder Farmers in India gives experimental evidence on the cooperation channel. Customer base environmental disclosure and supplier greenhouse gas emissions: A signaling theory perspective and Engaging Supply Chains in Climate Change cover disclosure-driven pressure. Sustainability in multi-tier supply chains: Understanding the double agency role of the first-tier supplier fits Nestlé's processor layer. Organizing Buyer‐Side Scope 3 Governance: Sensemaking in Supply Network Decarbonization frames the governance choice.

Patterns this week

Large-load contracts as screening devices for data-center risk

Almost every day brought a new instrument for deciding who bears the risk that data-center load is speculative or leaves early. The instruments included minimum terms and collateral (FERC filings), merged queue studies with cost shifts (PJM), separate tariffs and confidential minimum bills (Oklahoma), and coincident-peak charges that large users can avoid. Scoring thresholds (Korea) and procurement conditions (Spain) did the same job by other means. Because these designs vary across jurisdictions and are being adopted at different times, they offer a rare cross-sectional and staggered setting for studying how contract terms affect queue attrition, siting and cross-subsidies.

Long-lead capacity commitment under jump-type demand risk

Forecasts for AI load diverge sharply: the IEA projects a doubling by 2030, while Orennia models 191 GW at political risk. Community opposition doubled project stops, and permitting reform remains in legislative limbo. Generation, transmission, transformers and even low-carbon materials plants must be committed before demand resolves. This is a textbook setting for capacity investment with demand learning and regime switches. It is also an empirical test of whose forecasts are biased: regulated utilities report no slowdown while merchant analysts model large downside.

Buyer-driven Scope 3 governance diversifies its levers

Within one week, large buyers used four distinct tools on suppliers: - Inditex co-funded a blended-finance fund for suppliers. - Nestlé threatened to exit dairy suppliers that miss its standards. - Tesco pushed 400 suppliers onto a shared emissions-reporting platform. - Meta bought book-and-claim certificates to finance new low-carbon materials plants. Meta also signalled it may revise its 2030 net-zero target as AI capex outruns its pledges. Comparing these levers (supplier development, supplier selection, information, and demand commitment) is a core open question for sustainable-operations research.

Partial-coverage rules invite diversion and gaming

Many of this week's rules cover only part of the activity they target, which creates predictable leakage channels: - the 10 MW exemption in Korea, an invitation to bunch just below it; - 50% coverage of international voyages in the UK ETS, an incentive to restructure port calls; - the EU plastic waste ban, which still allows exports to OECD countries and could reroute trade through them; - uneven national enforcement of the EUDR, a route for importers through weakly policed member states; - Washington recycled-content fines small enough to treat as a cost of business; - the 20% capex threshold in SFDR 2.0, an invitation to cluster just above it. Each comes with a dated effective date, an advantage for difference-in-differences or bunching designs. The shared threat is that the diversion itself is hard to observe.
