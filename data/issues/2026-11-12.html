01 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Democrats take control of Georgia's utility regulator, setting up a challenge to Georgia Power's ~10 GW data-center gas buildout

Canary Media · Nov 11 · Read · ☆ Star

In the November 2026 election, Democrats won every statewide race in Georgia, including two Public Service Commission (PSC) seats. Republicans had held a PSC majority for 30 years; from January, three of the five commissioners will be Democrats. Commissioner-elect Shelia Edwards and reelected Commissioner Peter Hubbard say their top priority is slowing Georgia Power bill increases, which likely means challenging the utility's gas expansion for data centers.

BackgroundIn December 2025 the Republican-led PSC approved Georgia Power's plan for nearly 10 GW of new gas plants and batteries to serve proposed data centers. Commission staff had recommended approving only about a third of that right away. The Southern Environmental Law Center estimates the five new gas plants could cost customers $50-60 billion over their lifetimes. The PSC has approved six rate hikes in recent years, and typical residential bills are up 33%, or more than $500 a year. In February 2026 the PSC voted 3-2 not to reconsider the buildout. In April, Georgia Power requested another 2-6 GW of 'dispatchable capacity.' In August, the three Republican commissioners approved a contract to serve OpenAI's planned 3.2 GW data center; critics said its cost-recovery terms were not made public.
ProblemLong-lived generation capacity is being committed against data-center load forecasts that may not materialize, and residential ratepayers could carry the cost if they don't. The new majority may reopen the December decision and contest the April request of up to 6 GW. That creates regulatory uncertainty for assets the utility has already started planning. No specific votes or timelines have been confirmed.
Where & whyThe problem sits in the integrated resource planning and rate-case stage, where a regulated monopoly proposes capacity and the commission decides what costs can be recovered. Three frictions drive it. First, information asymmetry: the utility and data-center developers know more about how firm the load is than regulators do. Second, misaligned incentives: a rate-of-return utility earns on the capital it builds. Third, opacity in large-load contracts makes it hard to check Georgia Power's claim that data centers pay their own way. Political turnover adds uncertainty to commitments that last decades.
Stakeholders
Georgia Power — Wants recovery on approved gas plants and approval of 2-6 GW more; faces a possible reopening of its plans.
Residential ratepayers — Already paying 33% more; at risk of $50-60B in plant costs if data-center load underdelivers.
Data center developers (e.g., OpenAI's 3.2 GW site) — Need firm, timely power; their contract terms may be renegotiated or scrutinized.
New Democratic PSC majority — Promised bill relief; must decide which approvals to revisit without destabilizing reliability.
Environmental advocates (e.g., SELC) — Want to limit gas lock-in and get cost-recovery terms disclosed.
Research hints
💡 Treat the change in commission control as an event: compare Georgia Power's capacity filings and requests before and after with those of utilities in states whose regulators did not turn over.
💡 A capacity-expansion-under-uncertainty model in which the regulator, not the utility, bears political risk; ask how the threat of future disallowance should change the timing and size of investment (option value of waiting).
💡 Large-load tariff design as a contracting problem: which contract terms (minimum bills, exit fees, collateral) actually shift stranded-asset risk onto data centers?
Data leadsGeorgia PSC dockets (IRP, rate cases, large-load contracts), EIA-860 planned generator additions, EIA-861 residential prices and bills, LBNL interconnection queue data.
Keywordsdata center electricity demand · large-load tariffs · capacity investment under uncertainty · regulatory uncertainty · gas turbines
Related research
That's Not Fair: Tariff Structures for Electricity Markets with Rooftop Solar (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2017)
Studies how utility tariff structures shift costs between customer classes, the same fairness question raised by whether data-center capacity costs fall on residential ratepayers.
02 · Critical Minerals & Batteries

BMW signs LG Energy Solution and Samsung SDI as regional cell suppliers for its Gen6 EV batteries in North America and Europe

Supply Chain Dive · Nov 11 · Read · ☆ Star

BMW said on Nov. 11, 2026 that LG Energy Solution will supply Gen6 battery cells for its EVs in North America, and Samsung SDI will supply cells for EVs built in Europe, both starting in the late 2020s. Samsung SDI joins China's CATL and EVE Energy as BMW's third European cell supplier. Contract values, volumes, exact start dates and which LGES US plants will make the cells were not disclosed.

BackgroundBMW follows a 'local-for-local' strategy: cells are made near its five battery-assembly plants in Irlbach-Straßkirchen (Germany), Debrecen (Hungary), Shenyang (China), Woodruff (South Carolina) and San Luis Potosí (Mexico). It is spending about €650 million upgrading Plant Munich for Neue Klasse EVs. It also runs in-house competence centers in Germany for cell technology (Munich), cell manufacturing (Parsdorf) and cell recycling (Salching). BMW's purchasing head for high-voltage batteries says the network is meant to give 'resilient, flexible and competitive' cell supply.
ProblemAutomakers must decide how many cell suppliers to use per region, and from which countries, while trade policy keeps changing. In Europe, BMW is adding a Korean supplier alongside two Chinese ones. In North America it is relying on a Korean supplier. Each choice trades off cost, technology access, policy exposure and the bargaining power that comes with dual sourcing.
Where & whyThe decision sits in upstream sourcing of the highest-value EV component. Cell plants are capital-intensive and specific to each relationship, so contracts are long-term and switching is slow. Trade policy and origin rules penalize some supplier countries in some markets. Logistics and tariff costs push production to sit next to assembly. Concentration in Chinese cell makers adds supplier-region risk.
Stakeholders
BMW — Secure, cost-competitive cells for Neue Klasse/Gen6 ramp-up while limiting regional and policy risk.
LG Energy Solution and Samsung SDI — Long-term volume that helps justify plant capacity in the US and Europe.
CATL and EVE Energy — Share of BMW's European cell volume now split with a third supplier.
US and EU policymakers — Want localized battery production and less reliance on particular supplier countries.
Research hints
💡 Compare how many cell suppliers each automaker uses by region, and from which countries, to see whether diversification follows tariff and origin-rule exposure or just production location.
💡 Dual-sourcing models with capacity reservation: how do long lead times for cell plants change the value of a second or third supplier?
💡 Is local-for-local a resilience strategy or a cost and tariff strategy? Look at whether firms localize the same way when policy pressure differs across regions.
Data leadsAutomaker and supplier 10-K/20-F and annual reports, plus Benchmark/BNEF cell-plant capacity data (commercial); public alternatives are USITC DataWeb and UN Comtrade imports of lithium-ion cells by origin.
Keywordssupplier diversification · long-term supply contracts · supplier region risk · reshoring · industrial policy
Related research
Diversify or Concentrate? Supply Chain Responses to Policy Uncertainty (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2025)
Tests empirically whether firms diversify or concentrate their sourcing when policy uncertainty rises, the choice BMW faces across regions.
Impact of the U.S.–China trade war on the operating performance of U.S. firms: The role of outsourcing and supply base complexity (Journal of Operations Management, 2022)
Shows how US-China tariffs affected firms depending on their outsourcing and supply-base complexity, useful context for trade-driven cell localization.
03 · Circular Economy

US PET bottle recycling rate falls to 29% in 2025 while recycled content in bottles reaches a record 22.9%

Waste Dive · Nov 11 · Read · ☆ Star

NAPCOR data show the US PET bottle recycling rate fell to 29% in 2025 from 30.2% in 2024, and the North American rate fell to 36% from 39.2%. At the same time, average recycled PET (rPET) content in US bottles rose to a record 22.9% from 20.7%. Imports supplied a record 39.1% of rPET used in the US and Canada, up from 35.5%.

BackgroundThe article calls imported rPET a major reason for a wave of US reclaimer closures, which cut domestic production capacity by 25%. Lightweighting is also changing the numbers. Coca-Cola's lighter bottle designs reached full market penetration in 2025, cutting many bottles from 21 g to 18.5 g. Recycled PET thermoforms rose 7.4% to a record 226 million pounds.
ProblemCollection rates and recycled content are moving in opposite directions. Brands can meet recycled-content goals with imported rPET even as domestic collection and reclaiming shrink. Because recycling rates are measured by weight, lighter bottles can make collection look lower than it is (Closed Loop Partners). Very light bottles may also be physically lost at materials recovery facilities (MRFs) and reclaimers.
Where & whyThe problem sits at three points. In sorting and reclaiming, lightweight bottles escape MRF equipment. In the secondary-material market, imports compete with domestic reclaimers on price, and capacity exits. In measurement, weight-based metrics confuse lighter packaging with less recycling. Recycled-content targets create demand for rPET but do not specify where it comes from, so domestic collection and domestic demand become disconnected.
Stakeholders
US reclaimers — Lost 25% of capacity; squeezed by imports and harder-to-capture lightweight bottles.
Beverage brands (e.g., Coca-Cola) — Lightweighting cuts resin cost and emissions while meeting recycled-content goals, but may weaken domestic collection.
MRFs and collection programs — Lower capture and revenue from very light bottles.
Policymakers designing recycled-content and EPR rules — Need metrics that separate real recycling from weight effects and from imported content.
NAPCOR and data users — Credibility of weight-based recycling rates as a policy metric.
Research hints
💡 A secondary-material supply model in which lightweighting cuts both the weight collected and the probability of capture; ask how much of the rate decline is measurement versus real loss.
💡 Consider whether recycled-content mandates should have a domestic-origin dimension; compare how imported content affects domestic reclaimer capacity.
💡 Puzzle: record recycled content alongside reclaimer closures. Where do recycled-content targets bind, and who captures the rent?
Data leadsNAPCOR annual PET recycling reports, EPA Facts and Figures on materials and waste, USITC DataWeb/UN Comtrade imports of recycled PET flake and pellets, state EPR/PRO reports.
Keywordsrecycled content · secondary materials · packaging regulation · extended producer responsibility · plant closure
Related research
Do E‐Waste Laws Create Behavioral Spillovers? Quasi‐Experimental Evidence from California (Production and Operations Management, 2020)
Uses a quasi-experiment to evaluate a recycling law's operational and behavioral effects, a template for assessing recycled-content or EPR policy on PET.
04 · Food & Agriculture

UK salad supplier Kanes Foods enters administration with 662 jobs lost after wastewater plant failure and drought

SSBCrack · Nov 11 · Read · ☆ Star

Kanes Foods, an Evesham, Worcestershire supplier of pre-packed salads, stir-fry mixes and short-shelf-life sauces to major UK supermarkets, has gone into administration and stopped all operations. The most specific figure across reports is 662 jobs lost. The joint administrators cite a June 2025 wastewater treatment plant failure that cost about £2mn and permanently raised operating costs, followed by a drought that badly cut fresh salad supply.

BackgroundPre-packed salad is perishable and has thin margins, and it depends on steady flows of fresh produce and on wastewater processing at the plant. Fruitnet/FPJ warned of knock-on effects for growers and other businesses. Coverage dates from around Nov. 9-11, 2026. The administrators' names, the supermarkets affected and any sale of the business were not confirmed in the reporting reviewed, which relied on search summaries.
ProblemAn operational failure at the plant and a climate-driven input shock hit in sequence, and together they pushed a tier-1 supplier into insolvency. Supermarket buyers now face sudden supply gaps, and upstream growers lose a customer. The case raises the question of who should absorb compounding shocks in buyer-dominated fresh-food supply chains.
Where & whyThe problem sits at the processing tier, between growers and retailers. The wastewater failure permanently raised fixed costs. The drought then cut and made more expensive the supply of perishable raw material, which cannot be buffered with inventory. Retailers hold strong bargaining power, which likely limits how much of these costs a supplier can pass on. The result is thin financial buffers and correlated risks that single-shock planning misses.
Stakeholders
Kanes Foods workers — 662 jobs lost.
UK supermarkets — Need replacement suppliers for short-shelf-life salad and sauce lines.
Salad growers — Lose a buyer and may be owed money; knock-on risk flagged by FPJ.
Administrators and creditors — Recover value through any sale of the business or its assets.
Research hints
💡 Compounding shocks: a supplier-default model in which a permanent cost increase reduces the buffer available to absorb a later supply shock, compared with each shock alone.
💡 Contrast fresh produce, where inventory cannot buffer, with categories that store well, to see how the time a supplier can survive a shock shapes insolvency risk.
💡 Buyer-supplier power and cost pass-through: did retail contract terms let the supplier recover drought-driven input costs?
Data leadsUK Companies House filings and Insolvency Service statistics for food manufacturers, combined with UK Met Office/Environment Agency drought and hydrological records.
Keywordsproduce supply chains · perishable inventory · climate adaptation · cost pass-through · plant closure
Related research
IMPACT OF DISASTERS ON FIRMS IN DIFFERENT SECTORS: IMPLICATIONS FOR SUPPLY CHAINS (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2010)
Examines how disasters affect firm performance across sectors using secondary data, relevant to linking droughts to supplier distress.
Cascading disruptions: Impact of modularity and nexus supplier predictions (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2024)
Models how a disruption at one supplier cascades through a network, the knock-on risk to growers and retailers flagged here.
Thread to pull

Georgia's gas buildout and BMW's cell contracts both lock in long-lived, relationship-specific capacity on top of demand forecasts and policy that may change. Georgia tests what happens when the regulator itself changes; BMW spreads its bet across suppliers by region. The PET and Kanes stories show the downside when commitments meet a shifting environment: domestic reclaimers lost capacity to imports, and a supplier's buffers were used up by two shocks in a row. A common lens is to ask who holds the stranded-capacity risk in each chain and whether contracts or regulation price that risk explicitly.

New in the journals

EXPRESS: The Effect of State-Mandated Employment Certificates on Child Labor Violations in the United States
Production and Operations Management

The number of minors in US child labor violations more than tripled from 2017 to 2025, and several states have recently weakened child labor laws. The paper asks whether state rules requiring employers to obtain school-issued employment certificates (usually needing a parent's signature) before hiring minors reduce violations. It responds to the claim that there is little evidence these rules work.

Like the Georgia regulator turnover, changes in state labor laws are policy shocks that can be studied as natural experiments, here for social sustainability and labor compliance in operations.
