01 · Supply Chain Resilience

Tentative deal for 250 ILWU Local 26 watchmen averts strike at Los Angeles–Long Beach port terminals

FreightWaves · Nov 21 · Read · ☆ Star
↳ Follow-up to Nov 19 · Strike by 250 security workers could halt terminals handling over half of LA-Lon

The Pacific Maritime Association (PMA) and ILWU Local 26 reached a tentative contract under federal mediation. The deal covers about 250 watchmen (security guards) at the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach. It came just before a 12:01 a.m. Nov. 21, 2026 deadline, after members authorized a strike in late October. Final terms are undisclosed, and members still must ratify the deal; the existing contract stays in force until the vote.

BackgroundThe previous contract expired in July 2025 and was kept alive by extensions; WWD reports three extensions starting from a Nov. 1 deadline. Two days before the deal, PMA said there was 'little-to-no movement from the union.' Before the deal, PMA had offered:<br>• a $3,200 one-time bonus;<br>• wage increases lifting average full-time watchman pay from more than $170,000 to $198,000;<br>• fully employer-paid healthcare for active workers and retirees, plus higher pensions.<br>None of these are confirmed terms of the final agreement. Local 26 provides security at six container terminals, which WWD says handle more than half the complex's cargo.
ProblemA bargaining unit of only 250 workers held leverage over most of the largest US container gateway. Thousands of longshore workers, marine clerks and foremen not party to the dispute could have honored a picket line. Shippers had to plan contingencies around a deadline that was extended repeatedly. The risk is not fully resolved until ratification.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the terminal-labor link of the port node. Labor contracts are fragmented across units, but work is complementary across them: picket-line solidarity turns a small unit into a single point of failure for the whole terminal. Repeated extensions and opaque bargaining create deadline uncertainty. Shippers must decide whether to pre-ship, reroute to other coasts or hold inventory before knowing the outcome.
Stakeholders
ILWU Local 26 watchmen — Wages, healthcare and pension gains; leverage depends on other unions honoring pickets.
Pacific Maritime Association (terminal employers) — Labor cost containment versus the cost of shutting six terminals.
Importers and beneficial cargo owners — Exposure to delays and contingency costs (rerouting, early shipping, safety stock) while the outcome is uncertain.
Other ILWU units (longshore, clerks, foremen) — Lost work if they honor a picket line; solidarity sets precedent for their own talks.
Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service / US Department of Labor — Avoiding a national supply-chain disruption through mediated settlement.
Research hints
💡 Weakest-link (O-ring) production: why can a tiny complementary labor input carry hold-up power over a whole terminal?
💡 Compare shipper behavior around repeated contract-extension deadlines with the 2014–15 West Coast and 2024 East/Gulf Coast labor episodes: do import volumes shift ahead of each deadline?
💡 Look for anticipatory diversion: did vessel calls or import share move away from LA/LB in October–November 2026, despite the strike never happening?
Data leadsNOAA AIS vessel tracks for LA/LB calls and anchorage queues; Census USA Trade Online port-level imports; Panjiva bill-of-lading data (commercial) for shipper-level rerouting; PMA and port monthly TEU statistics.
Keywordslabor disruption · port congestion · dwell time · collective bargaining
Related research
Freedom of Association and Collective Bargaining in Global Supply Chains: A Research Agenda (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2021)
Sets out a research agenda on freedom of association and collective bargaining in supply chains, a frame for analyzing union leverage at logistics chokepoints.
Estimating the Operational Impact of Container Inspections at International Ports (Management Science, 2010)
Simulates how inspection policies create congestion using container-movement data from large terminals; the approach could be adapted to model capacity loss from a partial terminal shutdown.
02 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Alabama Power files $160 million 2027 rate cut, crediting data center revenue; watchdog points to tax cut and formula

Inside Climate News · Nov 20 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov. 20, 2026, Alabama Power filed with the Alabama Public Service Commission (PSC) to cut rates by about $160 million in 2027, starting with January bills. The utility has 1.5 million customers, and a customer using 1,000 kWh a month would save more than $50 a year. The company says the main driver is expected revenue from large-load data center contracts, which it says must cover their full cost of service and contribute to existing system costs.

BackgroundAlabama Power operates under the Rate Stabilization and Equalization (RSE) formula, created by the PSC in 1982. Rates adjust each January based on the company's projections, with no formal rate case. The filing projects 2027 income of $1.76 billion, above the allowed return, which triggers the formula's reduction. Separately, the company will stop billing customers for part of its advertising budget, as the PSC asked, saving about 4 cents a month per average customer. An Inside Climate News investigation found Alabama Power customers paid the highest total electric bills in the US in 2024. The PSC had already approved a rate freeze through 2027, and Sen. Katie Britt has claimed credit for the cut.
ProblemIt is contested whether large data center loads lower or raise costs for other ratepayers. The utility credits data center contracts. Energy Alabama says the formula forced the cut, calling it 'handing back money it was on track to overcharge customers.' Inside Climate News reports the watchdog credits a federal tax cut, but that argument was not fully verified in our notes. The filing still needs PSC action.
Where & whyThe question sits in retail rate design and cost allocation between customer classes. Data center contract terms are typically not public, so outsiders cannot check whether large loads cover their incremental costs. Formula ratemaking, where revenue above the allowed return flows back to customers, makes it hard to separate the effect of new load from tax changes and other projection inputs. Political pressure over high bills gives the utility and politicians incentives to frame the cut favorably.
Stakeholders
Alabama Power — Justifying data center growth and the capacity it requires as beneficial to existing customers.
Residential and small business customers — About $50 a year in savings now; risk of bearing future capacity costs if large-load contracts underpay.
Data center operators — Access to power and contract terms; public legitimacy of their load growth.
Alabama Public Service Commission — Approving the filing and overseeing RSE outcomes under political scrutiny.
Energy Alabama and other consumer advocates — Transparency on whether cuts come from data centers, tax changes or over-earning.
Research hints
💡 Cost-causation and cross-subsidy tests: when does an incremental large load lower average rates? Think fixed-cost spreading versus new capacity needs.
💡 Decomposition puzzle: separate data center revenue, a federal tax change and over-earning within a formula rate. Compare with utilities under traditional rate cases.
💡 Cross-utility comparison: do utilities with large-load tariffs or recent data center contracts show different residential rate trajectories?
Data leadsAlabama PSC RSE filings and dockets; EIA-861 utility revenue, sales and customers by class; EIA-860 planned capacity additions in the Southern Company territory; Southern Company SEC 10-K filings for large-load contract disclosures.
Keywordsdata center electricity demand · large-load tariffs · cost pass-through · formula rate regulation
Related research
That's Not Fair: Tariff Structures for Electricity Markets with Rooftop Solar (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2017)
Analyzes fairness and cross-subsidies in electricity tariff structures when some customers (rooftop solar) change their grid use, the same allocation question raised by large data center loads.
03 · Circular Economy

EU ban on plastic waste exports to non-OECD countries takes effect Nov. 21, 2026

INSIGHT EU MONITORING · Nov 21 · Read · ☆ Star

Under the revised Waste Shipment Regulation, the EU's ban on exporting plastic waste to non-OECD countries took effect on Nov. 21, 2026. According to EUbusiness's account of the Commission announcement, it runs until at least May 21, 2029. Exports to OECD members remain allowed, but under stricter controls and Commission monitoring. A wider ban on other non-hazardous waste exports to non-OECD countries (paper, rubber, metal, glass) starts May 21, 2027.

BackgroundThe EU generates about 40 million tonnes of plastic waste a year. Export estimates vary: Reuters says about 1.5 Mt, the Commission gives 1–1.5 Mt, and BigMint says about 1.4 Mt in 2025, roughly half to non-OECD countries. Top destinations are Turkey, the UK, Malaysia, Indonesia and Vietnam. BigMint, a secondary market-intelligence source, estimates Malaysia, Indonesia and Vietnam took about 628,000 t in 2025. After 2029, non-OECD countries can apply to receive EU plastic waste if they show they manage it properly. Environment Commissioner Jessika Roswall tied the ban to the stalled UN plastics treaty talks, and a Circular Economy Act is expected in December.
ProblemRoughly hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year previously sent to Southeast Asia must now be recycled or disposed of in the EU, or shipped to OECD countries. It is unclear whether EU recycling capacity can absorb this volume. It is also unclear whether trade will be redirected through OECD countries such as Turkey, and how strictly those OECD exports will be policed.
Where & whyThe friction arises at the end-of-life sorting and export stage. Export had been a low-cost outlet for low-value mixed plastics. Domestic processing capacity and recycled-plastic prices determine whether the banned volume is recycled, incinerated or landfilled. Monitoring is imperfect and waste brokers know more than regulators about final destinations, so diversion and relabeling are plausible responses.
Stakeholders
EU waste collectors and brokers — Loss of a cheap export outlet; higher domestic treatment costs.
EU recyclers — More feedstock supply, but possibly lower gate fees and capacity strain.
Malaysia, Indonesia and Vietnam — Lost imports of feedstock, and less of the pollution burden that came with them.
OECD destinations (e.g., Turkey, UK) — Potential inflow of diverted volumes under tighter monitoring.
European Commission — Credibility of the waste-trade rules and leverage in plastics treaty talks.
Research hints
💡 Natural experiment on trade diversion: compare HS 3915 flows from the EU to non-OECD versus OECD partners before and after Nov. 21, 2026, and versus non-EU exporters.
💡 Watch for a pre-ban export surge in 2026, and for the staggered May 2027 ban on paper, metal and glass as a second shock.
💡 Treat-dump-export models with imperfect monitoring: does a destination ban shift volumes toward incineration or toward illegal channels?
Data leadsEurostat Comext and UN Comtrade for HS 3915 (plastic waste and scrap) by partner; national import statistics for Malaysia, Indonesia, Vietnam and Turkey; EU recycled-plastic price indices.
Keywordstrade diversion · secondary materials · regulatory enforcement · plastic waste trade
Related research
Treat, Dump, or Export? How Domestic and International Waste Management Policies Shape Waste Chain Outcomes (Management Science, 2023)
Models how domestic and international waste policies, under imperfect monitoring, shift decentralized waste chains between treating, dumping and exporting, directly relevant to how an export ban will play out.
Do E‐Waste Laws Create Behavioral Spillovers? Quasi‐Experimental Evidence from California (Production and Operations Management, 2020)
Offers quasi-experimental evidence on behavioral spillovers from e-waste laws, a template for evaluating waste regulation shocks empirically.
04 · Trade & Industrial Policy

Ford reopens retooled Oakville, Ontario plant to build Super Duty pickups despite 25% US tariff

ET Manufacturing · Nov 22 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov. 20, 2026, Ford reopened its Oakville, Ontario assembly plant to build Super Duty pickups. The Globe and Mail names the F-250 and F-350 and gives annual capacity of 100,000 units. Reported investment figures conflict: AFP says US$3.5 billion, while the Globe says about $5 billion. Government support figures also differ across sources: C$464 million combined federal and Ontario, a $464M federal grant, or $760M.

BackgroundOakville built the Ford Edge until mid-2024. It then closed to retool for electric vehicles, before the plan changed to trucks. The US applies a 25% tariff on Canadian-made vehicles. US–Canada trade talks broke off in August and have recently resumed. On Nov. 9, Stellantis confirmed it is selling its Brampton plant, which employed nearly 3,000 people, to a defense company; PM Mark Carney called that a 'betrayal.' Ford Canada CEO Bev Goodman stressed the value of an integrated North American manufacturing network. The number of jobs restored at Oakville was not verified.
ProblemFord is committing large-volume truck capacity in Canada even though output likely sold mainly in the US faces a 25% tariff, while a rival exits Canadian assembly. The choice weighs:<br>• sunk retooling investment;<br>• government subsidies;<br>• labor commitments;<br>• network integration;<br>• tariffs that may change with the trade talks.
Where & whyThe decision sits at capacity siting and allocation within a cross-border production network. Tariff policy is highly uncertain, and plant conversion is irreversible and slow. Firms must therefore weigh option value against current tariff costs. Subsidies from Canadian governments and political pressure further distort the margin between the US and Canada.
Stakeholders
Ford — Recovering the retooling investment and meeting Super Duty demand while absorbing or avoiding tariff costs.
Canadian federal and Ontario governments — Retaining auto jobs and footprint; return on subsidies; leverage in trade talks.
Oakville workers and local suppliers — Restored employment and orders, contingent on tariff outcomes.
US policymakers — Tariffs intended to pull assembly into the US are being tested by this decision.
Stellantis and Brampton workers — Contrasting exit decision; about 3,000 jobs affected by the plant sale.
Research hints
💡 Real options under policy uncertainty: when does keeping or expanding offshore capacity dominate reshoring when tariffs could reverse?
💡 Contrast Ford Oakville (reopen) with Stellantis Brampton (sell): what firm-level factors, such as product mix, margins and subsidy terms, explain divergent responses to the same tariff?
💡 Note the source discrepancies on investment and subsidy amounts; disentangling subsidy stacking is itself an empirical problem.
Data leadsUSITC DataWeb and Census USA Trade Online imports of Canadian light trucks by tariff line; Ford SEC 10-K/10-Q segment and capex disclosures; Statistics Canada vehicle production and trade data.
Keywordsfacility siting · industrial policy · capacity investment under uncertainty · reshoring
Related research
Shock and Awe: A Theoretical Framework and Data Sources for Studying the Impact of 2025 Tariffs on Global Supply Chains (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2025)
Provides a framework and data sources for studying the 2025 US tariffs as a supply-chain shock, directly applicable to automaker siting responses.
Flexibility Value of Reshoring Capacity Under Policy Uncertainty and Domestic Competition (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2025)
Models the flexibility value of onshore versus offshore capacity under policy uncertainty, the core trade-off in Ford's Oakville decision.
05 · ESG & Disclosure

European Commission opens infringement cases against Bulgaria, Greece, Malta and Poland over EUDR enforcement authorities

Wood Central · Nov 21 · Read · ☆ Star

On Nov. 19, 2026, the European Commission sent letters of formal notice to Bulgaria, Greece, Malta and Poland, the first step in infringement procedures. The four failed to designate and/or notify competent authorities under the EU Deforestation Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2023/1115). Each has two months to reply. Wood Central notes the EUDR is due to apply to large and medium companies in under six weeks, implying about Dec. 30, 2026 (not confirmed).

BackgroundMember states had until Dec. 30, 2023 to designate authorities that check operators and traders, order corrective measures and impose penalties. Bulgaria designated authorities for every covered commodity except rubber. Greece, Malta and Poland failed to designate authorities, failed to notify the Commission, or both. Wood Central reports Poland told its parliament there was 'no such thing as legal uncertainty' over the EUDR; this was not checked against the full article.
ProblemEUDR obligations bind companies EU-wide, but checks and penalties depend on national authorities, some of which do not yet exist weeks before application. Uneven enforcement could make compliance costs, and the risk of penalties, differ by entry country. It is uncertain whether the four states will comply within two months, or whether the Commission will escalate to a reasoned opinion or a Court of Justice referral.
Where & whyThe gap is at the enforcement layer of a due-diligence regime: an EU rule implemented by national competent authorities. Member states have uneven capacity and willingness to enforce, which creates a coordination problem. Operators may route deforestation-risk commodities (e.g., rubber, wood) through weakly enforced ports or member states. Firms that invest in multi-tier traceability may find rivals free-riding where checks are lax.
Stakeholders
European Commission — Credible, uniform EUDR enforcement before the application date.
Bulgaria, Greece, Malta, Poland — Avoiding escalation to a reasoned opinion or Court referral; administrative cost of building authorities.
Operators and traders importing covered commodities — Compliance costs and penalty exposure that may vary by entry country; level playing field.
Upstream producers and smallholders in sourcing countries — Market access depends on how strictly traceability is checked.
Research hints
💡 Regulatory arbitrage lens: under decentralized enforcement, do import flows of EUDR commodities shift toward member states without functioning authorities?
💡 Compare with other EU rules enforced by national authorities (e.g., the EU Timber Regulation, its predecessor) to see how uneven enforcement shaped compliance.
💡 Principal–agent setup: the Commission depends on member-state agents with differing incentives to enforce.
Data leadsEurostat Comext imports of EUDR commodities (wood, rubber, cocoa, coffee, soy, palm oil, cattle) by member state of entry; the Commission's infringement decisions database; Trase deforestation-risk supply chain data.
Keywordsdeforestation · regulatory enforcement · mandatory due diligence · multi-tier traceability
Related research
Toward “Omniscient” supply chains: Enabling and incentivizing sustainable operations (Production and Operations Management, 2026)
Discusses the structural mismatch between value-chain due diligence demands and the limits of regulatory enforcement, central to the EUDR enforcement gap.
Sustainability in multi‐tier supply chains: Understanding the double agency role of the first‐tier supplier (Journal of Operations Management, 2015)
Studies when first-tier suppliers act as agents of lead firms in enforcing sustainability compliance deep in multi-tier chains, the traceability mechanism EUDR relies on.
Thread to pull

The EU plastic-waste ban and the EUDR infringement cases share a structure: EU-wide rules whose effect depends on uneven national monitoring, which opens room for diversion through weakly policed channels (OECD intermediaries, member states without authorities). Comparing leakage under these two regimes, perhaps with trade data from Eurostat Comext, could show when decentralized enforcement undermines supply-chain regulation. A similar 'who really bears the cost' question links the Alabama Power cut and Ford's subsidized Oakville reopening: in both, public bodies shape private capacity decisions under contested accounting.

New in the journals

EXPRESS: Estimating censored demand in family planning supply chains: forecast accuracy, inventory implications, and public health outcomes
Production and Operations Management

When stockouts hide true demand, forecasts are biased low and reinforce understocking in family planning supply chains. The authors propose the Truncated Conformal Kalman Filter, combining a Tobit Kalman filter with conformal prediction, which explicitly distinguishes between different censoring mechanisms. They evaluate it on forecast accuracy, inventory implications and public health outcomes.

Censored-demand bias also matters whenever disruptions such as port strikes or export bans truncate observed flows, which complicates the before-and-after trade comparisons suggested in today's items.

Production Rate Control in Condition-Based Maintenance Planning
M&SOM

The paper studies integrated condition-based maintenance and production control for assets whose deterioration speeds up with the production rate. Running harder raises revenue but accelerates wear, so production and maintenance decisions should be coordinated with the asset's condition.

The same throughput-versus-wear trade-off applies to newly retooled plants like Ford Oakville and to power generation assets pushed harder by data center load growth.
