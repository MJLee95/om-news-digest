01 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Virginia utilities file first public grid-utilization metrics: Dominion's distribution grid averages 41% use as data center congestion warnings surge

Latitude Media · Oct 21 · Read · ☆ Star

Under a new Virginia law, Dominion Energy and Appalachian Power (APCo) filed the first public measures of distribution-grid utilization with the State Corporation Commission (SCC). Dominion reported 41% average annual use (July 2025–2026) and about 80% during rare peak hours. APCo reported 14% annually and 54% at peak. Separately, Dominion's most severe transmission congestion warnings rose from 8 in 2023 to more than 172 so far this year, covering 2,530 hours and concentrated in Loudoun and Prince William counties.

BackgroundDistribution grids are sized for rare local peaks, so much capacity sits idle most of the year. Clean-energy group Deploy Action helped write the Virginia law, which directs the SCC to use utilization figures to judge whether alternatives to new poles and wires would be faster or cheaper. Those alternatives include solar, storage and flexible demand. Dominion serves northern Virginia's 'Data Center Alley'. APCo serves mostly rural Southwest Virginia, where demand is falling. Dominion built its transmission measures with EPRI.
ProblemThe dispute is over how the metrics should be used. Dominion wants them as a screening tool rather than a 'deciding factor' in rejecting spending. It also wants to watch trends for 3–5 years before making any metric binding. Deploy Action argues this conflicts with the law's purpose, that the utilities undervalue distributed resources, and that five years is too long. Measurement choices matter too. Dominion's 'general' metric counts customer solar and storage as capacity, while its 'planning' metric does not. Grid-wide averages can also hide constrained feeders and substations.
Where & whyThe tension sits in distribution and transmission capacity planning, the stage where regulators approve utility capital spending. Utilities know far more about feeder-level conditions than regulators do. Utilities also earn returns on capital, which may bias them toward building over using existing headroom or flexibility. Large data center loads concentrate demand in a few counties, so local constraints can bind even when system-wide utilization is low.
Stakeholders
Dominion Energy — Wants to keep discretion over grid investment and avoid binding metrics that could block capital spending.
Virginia SCC — Must decide how far utilization data should shape approval of grid spending, without final metrics yet in place.
Deploy Action and Utilize Coalition — Want binding, feeder- or substation-level metrics that favor solar, storage and flexible demand over new wires.
Data center developers in Loudoun and Prince William counties — Face transmission constraints and shrinking safety margins that could delay new large-load interconnections.
Ratepayers, including APCo's rural customers — Pay for grid investment that may be underused (14% annual utilization at APCo).
Research hints
💡 Think about the gap between 41% average and roughly 80% peak utilization as a peak-load pricing and capacity-sizing problem, and ask what flexible large loads could change.
💡 Utility–regulator information asymmetry and rate-of-return incentives suggest a principal–agent lens on how utilization metrics get defined (general vs. planning).
💡 The jump from 8 to 172+ congestion warnings, concentrated in two counties, could serve as an outcome for studying how localized data center clustering stresses transmission.
Data leadsVirginia SCC docket filings (utilization metrics), EIA-861 utility sales, PJM load and capacity data, and LBNL interconnection queue data.
Keywordsdata center electricity demand · grid utilization metrics · capacity investment under uncertainty · large-load tariffs · demand response
Related research
Electricity Time‐of‐Use Tariff with Stochastic Demand (Production and Operations Management, 2016)
Models time-of-use versus flat tariffs under stochastic demand, which bears on using price signals to shift load from the peak hours that drive grid sizing.
02 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Memphis council rejects 18-month data center moratorium but passes 135-day pause

DatacenterDynamics · Oct 21 · Read · ☆ Star

On October 20, 2026, after roughly 5.5 hours of debate, the Memphis City Council split 6–6 (one member absent) on Councilor JB Smiley Jr.'s proposed data center moratorium of up to 18 months, so it failed. The council then passed a substitute: a 135-day pause on new data center applications, meant to align with Mayor Paul Young's 90-day impact study ordered in early October. Some sources describe the pause as covering construction rather than applications.

BackgroundMemphis has become a contested site for AI computing; DCD's coverage links the debate to Elon Musk's AI company. An October 6 meeting drew more than 100 people, mostly opponents, and reportedly ended in protests and fights, which postponed the vote. Action News 5 reported 407 people in the chamber for the vote. The rejected plan would have allowed permanent zoning rules, required applicants to disclose information, and studied air, grid, water and noise impacts. It would not have stopped existing data centers.
ProblemThe city must balance AI infrastructure investment against local environmental and community impacts. It has to do so before it has impact evidence or a permanent zoning framework. A short pause buys study time but leaves long-run rules unsettled. Some councilors said they received the substitute text at short notice, which raises questions about procedural legitimacy.
Where & whyThe friction arises at local siting and permitting, where land-use authority over large loads sits. Costs such as air, water, noise and grid strain fall on nearby residents, while benefits flow to firms and the wider economy. Regulators lack information on impacts, which is why the rejected plan sought disclosure requirements. Short, uncertain pauses add regulatory uncertainty to developers' siting choices.
Stakeholders
Memphis City Council and Mayor Paul Young — Need to produce an impact study and permanent rules within roughly 90–135 days while managing intense public opposition.
Local residents and community groups — Want protection from air, water, noise and grid impacts and more disclosure from data center operators.
Data center developers, including Musk-linked AI operations — Face delays to new projects and uncertainty about future zoning; existing sites are not covered.
Neighboring jurisdictions — Could gain projects redirected away from Memphis during the pause.
Research hints
💡 Local moratoria can be studied as regulatory arbitrage: do projects shift to adjacent counties or states, and how fast?
💡 Treat the 135-day pause, a short and explicitly temporary shock, as distinct from an indefinite ban, using real-options models of siting under regulatory uncertainty.
💡 The 6–6 split and the substitute vote are a close-call setting; compare cities that adopted moratoria with those that narrowly did not.
Data leadsMemphis City Council agendas and ordinances, local permit and zoning applications, GDELT news coverage of data center moratoria across US cities, and EIA-860 for new generation tied to large loads.
Keywordsfacility siting · data center electricity demand · regulatory uncertainty · siting moratorium
Related research
Household Credit and Regulatory Arbitrage: Evidence from Online Marketplace Lending (Management Science, 2022)
Gives evidence that tightening a regulation in some cities pushes activity into other channels, the same kind of displacement a local data center pause may cause across jurisdictions.
Exploring retailers’ sensitivity to local sustainability policies (Journal of Operations Management, 2007)
Studies how local sustainability-motivated regulations change firms' logistics and operating choices, a template for analyzing local siting rules.
03 · Trade & Industrial Policy

LevelTen: solar PPA benchmark rises 4.9% in Q3 2026 as buyers rush ahead of Section 232 tariffs

Utility Dive · Oct 21 · Read · ☆ Star

LevelTen's Q3 2026 index shows its continental 25th-percentile (P25) solar PPA price up 4.9%. Its new Core-Markets P25 benchmark stands at $64.23/MWh, up 6% year over year and 12.7% since Q3 2024. Wind P25 prices were flat for the quarter but up 13.8% year over year and 27.5% since 2024. LevelTen attributes the solar rise mainly to Section 232 tariffs announced in early August and taking effect in December.

BackgroundThe tariffs cover the whole solar supply chain: polysilicon, ingots, wafers, cells and modules. They combine a 15% blanket tariff with a minimum import price, which LevelTen says raises imported module prices by more than 40%. LevelTen expects the tariffs to add $3–7/MWh to PPA prices. Wind remains held back by federal permitting problems. Note that $64.23 is a P25 benchmark first published this quarter, not a true average, so comparisons with earlier quarters may not match exactly.
ProblemCorporate renewable buyers and developers must sign long-term contracts while the cost of trade policy is still uncertain. Much of Q3's increase reflects buyers rushing to sign before the tariff start date. Stockpiling modules is not allowed under current rules, and more than half of modules have already been repriced. How far prices will rise once the tariffs take effect is unknown.
Where & whyPass-through happens at the procurement link between module importers, developers and offtakers. Tariffs and minimum import prices raise equipment costs, which developers push into PPA strike prices. Uncertainty about timing encourages buyers to sign early, and restrictions on stockpiling limit the usual inventory hedge.
Stakeholders
Corporate PPA buyers — Face higher strike prices ($3–7/MWh tariff impact) and must decide whether to lock in contracts now or wait.
Solar developers — Need to reprice projects and secure modules without stockpiling.
Commerce Department — Administers the Section 232 tariffs and enforces against stockpiling.
Domestic solar manufacturers — Gain protection from import competition across the supply chain.
Research hints
💡 The gap between announcement (August) and effective date (December) is a classic setting for pre-buy behavior; compare contract timing and volumes before and after.
💡 Use a VPPA portfolio or MDP lens to ask how corporations meeting renewable targets should time procurement when input-cost shocks are anticipated.
💡 Contrast solar (tariff-driven) with wind (permitting-driven) price paths to separate trade effects from other policy frictions.
Data leadsLevelTen PPA Price Index, USITC DataWeb imports of modules and cells by tariff line, the Federal Register notice for the Section 232 action, and LBNL queue data on project timing.
Keywordspre-buy behavior · industrial policy · price floors · power purchase agreements · long-term supply contracts
Related research
Meeting Corporate Renewable Power Targets (Management Science, 2022)
Models how firms meet renewable targets using rolling portfolios of virtual PPAs, directly relevant to how buyers respond to tariff-driven price jumps.
04 · Supply Chain Resilience

Apple commits up to $100M to Pennsylvania titanium supplier and expands film deal with Qnity under its US manufacturing program

Supply Chain Dive · Oct 21 · Read · ☆ Star

On October 20, 2026, Apple named new and expanded partners in its American Manufacturing Program (AMP) for the foldable iPhone Duo and the iPhone 18 Pro. Apple commits up to $100 million over three years to Perryman Company, a family-owned Pennsylvania titanium producer. Perryman will melt, forge and roll grade 5 titanium with at least 80% recycled content for the Duo's casing. Apple also expanded its partnership with Delaware-based Qnity Electronics for advanced films used in the 18 Pro's variable-aperture camera; no dollar figure was given.

BackgroundThe announcements fall under Apple's four-year US manufacturing commitment, now $600 billion. AppleInsider reports that it began at $500 billion in 2025 and was later raised. Qnity joined AMP in March 2026, according to Pulse2. Other firms, including Broadcom, Corning, Coherent, TSMC and Amkor, appear to be existing partners, but their roles are not confirmed. COO Sabih Khan is quoted in the release.
ProblemLead firms are shifting parts of their materials and components supply to domestic producers through multiyear funding commitments. This raises questions about the costs, how resilient the new arrangements really are, and how far such deals go beyond signaling to policymakers. Using recycled titanium also ties reshoring to secondary material supply.
Where & whyThe decision sits at upstream materials and components sourcing. Policy uncertainty and concentration risk in offshore supply push buyers toward domestic suppliers. Smaller domestic suppliers, such as a family-owned titanium firm, need guaranteed demand and capital before investing in new capacity, so the buyer commits funding up front.
Stakeholders
Apple — Seeks supply security and policy goodwill, and must deliver on its $600B pledge.
Perryman Company — Gains up to $100M in committed demand but becomes dependent on one large buyer.
Qnity Electronics — Gains volume for specialized camera films.
US policymakers — Want visible domestic manufacturing investment from large firms.
Titanium scrap and recycling suppliers — Face new demand from the at-least-80% recycled-content requirement.
Research hints
💡 Event-study logic applies: how does the market value supplier-level reshoring announcements, and do suppliers react differently from the buyer?
💡 Buyer-funded capacity at small suppliers fits supply-contract and capacity-investment models; think about who bears demand risk over the three years.
💡 A recycled-content requirement in reshored supply is a puzzle for closed-loop supply chain work: where does the scrap come from?
Data leadsApple newsroom releases and Supplier List, SEC EDGAR 10-K/8-K filings, FactSet Revere links (public alternative: Compustat customer segments), and USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries for titanium.
Keywordssupplier diversification · secondary materials · long-term supply contracts · reshoring · industrial policy
Related research
Return to the United States: Impact of Reshoring Announcements and Reshoring Risks on Market Valuation (Management Science, 2024)
Estimates how reshoring announcements and their risks affect market value, directly applicable to AMP supplier announcements.

Case-studies why firms locate production in high-cost countries, offering a framework for Apple's domestic sourcing decisions.
05 · Circular Economy

USGBC launches TRUE v2 zero-waste certification with reweighted scoring and a 100-point scale

ESG Dive · Oct 21 · Read · ☆ Star

Around October 20, 2026, the US Green Building Council launched TRUE v2 (Total Resource Use and Efficiency), a zero-waste certification for facilities, construction projects and events. The update streamlines requirements, gives more credit to harder, higher-impact actions, rebalances points to help newcomers, and raises maximum points from 81 to 100 (per Facilities Dive). USGBC says the roughly 10-year-old program has more than 680 certified projects that have diverted 8.9 million tons of waste from disposal.

BackgroundTRUE requirements cover waste diversion, data collection, staff training and community impact. USGBC cites rising disposal costs, shrinking landfill space and growing waste volumes as drivers. Version 2 was developed with USGBC members, the TRUE Advisory Council and a global public consultation, and is worded to work internationally. Colgate-Palmolive's chief sustainability officer, Ann Tracy, is quoted in the launch.
ProblemVoluntary certifications face a trade-off. Making them easier attracts more adopters but risks diluting their meaning, while rewarding high performers keeps them credible. Changing the point scale and weights also makes v1 and v2 scores hard to compare. It is unclear whether certification produces real waste reductions or mainly documents existing practice.
Where & whyThe friction is in voluntary third-party certification. Outsiders cannot easily verify a facility's waste performance, so certification serves as a signal, and its design decides who adopts and whether a pass reflects real effort. Rising landfill costs change the business case on the adoption side.
Stakeholders
USGBC — Wants wider adoption and international reach while keeping the label credible.
Facility owners and event organizers — Weigh certification and documentation costs against disposal savings and reputational benefits.
Consumer brands such as Colgate-Palmolive — Use certification to substantiate zero-waste claims.
Waste haulers and recyclers — See shifts in waste volumes away from landfill.
Research hints
💡 Use a certification-design lens (signaling, isomorphism) to ask whether lowering entry barriers changes the type of adopter.
💡 The v1-to-v2 change is a standard revision that could be studied like ISO version transitions: adoption timing and early-mover effects.
💡 Compare certified projects' reported diversion with local disposal costs to see whether economics or signaling drives adoption.
Data leadsUSGBC TRUE certified project directory, EPA materials, waste and recycling facts and figures, and state landfill tipping-fee data.
Keywordszero waste certification · voluntary disclosure · secondary materials · sustainability standards
Related research
A competitive advantage from the implementation timing of ISO management standards (Journal of Operations Management, 2015)
Shows how the timing of adopting management standards affects competitive advantage, a frame for early versus late TRUE v2 adopters.
Administrative environmental innovations, supply network structure, and environmental disclosure (Journal of Operations Management, 2020)
Links administrative environmental tools, like waste tracking and certification, to firms' environmental disclosure.
Thread to pull

The Virginia utilization filings and the Memphis pause are two views of the same constraint. In Virginia, regulators are getting their first numbers on spare grid capacity versus local congestion, while Memphis is pausing applications because it lacks impact information. Meanwhile, LevelTen's tariff-driven PPA increases raise the cost of the clean power data centers often contract for. One idea worth exploring is how measured grid headroom, local siting rules and procurement costs together shape where AI loads end up.

A Decision-Support Framework for Food Bank Operations: Overcoming Waste and Uncertainty in Food Distribution
M&SOM

The paper develops forward-looking allocation policies for food banks that must distribute uncertain, perishable donations. The policies balance effectiveness (nutrition and freshness relative to need), efficiency (volume distributed and waste avoided) and equity, where myopic rules tend to fall short.

It is a methodological cousin of the circular-economy and waste themes in today's TRUE v2 item, showing how dynamic allocation under supply uncertainty can be framed as a waste-reduction problem.
