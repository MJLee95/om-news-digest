01 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Irish court challenge to regulator's data-centre grid-connection policy keeps siting rules in limbo

Bloomberg.com · Nov 15 · Read · ☆ Star

Bloomberg (15 Nov 2026) examined an ongoing judicial review against Ireland's energy regulator, the Commission for Regulation of Utilities (CRU). Friends of the Irish Environment, Friends of the Earth Ireland and ClientEarth brought the case. They are challenging the CRU's December 2025 Large Energy Users Connection Policy, which ended a de facto moratorium on new data-centre grid connections around Dublin that had been in place since 2021. The High Court allowed the case to proceed on 27 April 2026 and accepted the State as a Notice Party; no hearing date or ruling has been reported.

BackgroundIreland has about 89–90 data centres. They used about 23% of metered electricity in 2025, up from 5% a decade earlier, and ClientEarth says their share could reach a third by 2030. The 2025 policy reopened connections on conditions. New data centres need on-site generation or batteries able to cover their full demand, and must supply power back to the grid when needed. TikTok dropped plans for another Irish data centre in April, and about 2,000 people joined data-centre protests in Dublin on 7 Nov 2026.
ProblemThe groups argue the policy lets data centres run on fossil fuels for six years. After that, they must source at least 80% renewable supply, but backup generation does not count toward that share. Reports citing the Irish Times say up to 20% of new data centres' energy could come from non-renewable sources. The groups claim this breaches Ireland's 2015 Climate Action Act, the EU Energy Efficiency and Strategic Environmental Assessment Directives, and the Espoo, Kiev Protocol and Aarhus conventions. A ruling against the CRU could void or reshape the rules under which firms are deciding where to site capacity.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the grid-connection stage. A constrained transmission system near Dublin has to absorb large, inflexible loads. The regulator shifted adequacy risk onto data centres by requiring self-supply, but self-supply is often fossil-fuelled, which puts grid reliability in tension with climate law. Litigation adds regulatory uncertainty on top of an existing capacity shortfall.
Stakeholders
Data-centre operators and hyperscalers — Need connection certainty for multi-year siting and investment decisions in Ireland, or must move capacity elsewhere.
CRU (energy regulator) — Must keep the grid reliable while defending the legality of its connection policy.
Environmental NGOs (FIE, FoE Ireland, ClientEarth) — Want data-centre growth bound by Ireland's climate obligations and by environmental assessment.
Irish State and households — Weigh foreign investment and tax revenue against emissions targets, electricity prices and public opposition.
Research hints
💡 Treat the 2021 moratorium, the December 2025 reopening and a possible court ruling as successive siting shocks. Compare Ireland with other data-centre hubs that have tightened connection rules.
💡 Consider a capacity-investment-under-uncertainty model in which a firm can wait, build with on-site backup, or relocate. How does litigation risk change the value of waiting?
💡 Puzzle: what does mandatory on-site self-supply do to total system emissions compared with grid supply? Storage-arbitrage studies offer a template.
Data leadsCSO Ireland data-centre metered electricity statistics; EirGrid generation capacity statements; Irish High Court case records; ClientEarth and CRU decision documents.
Keywordsinterconnection policy · siting moratorium · litigation risk · data center electricity demand · regulatory uncertainty
Related research
The Unintended Carbon Impacts of Large-Scale Electricity Storage (Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, 2025)
Shows how storage operations indirectly change market-level carbon emissions, which bears on whether battery- or generator-backed self-supply for data centres is actually clean.
Modeling the Impact of Market Interventions on the Strategic Evolution of Electricity Markets (Operations Research, 2008)
Models how market interventions reshape generation portfolios over the long run, useful for thinking about how connection rules change investment in Ireland's grid.
02 · Energy & AI Data Centers

Georgia Power's 20% battery capacity credit tilts its 2–6 GW data-centre-driven buildout toward gas, advocates say

Inside Climate News · Nov 14 · Read · ☆ Star
↳ Follow-up to Nov 12 · Democrats take control of Georgia's utility regulator, setting up a challenge to

Georgia Power plans to add 2,000–6,000 MW of capacity for 2032–2033, driven by data centres and electrification, with gas and battery storage among the options. According to Southern Environmental Law Center attorney Bob Sherrier, the utility is crediting batteries with only 20% Effective Load Carrying Capability (ELCC) in this procurement. Its earlier calculations imply about 75%. Selected projects go to regulators for approval in mid-2027.

BackgroundELCC measures how much of a resource's nameplate capacity counts as reliable at peak demand. Inside Climate News gives typical values of about 60–80% for gas, 4–40% for solar or wind without storage, and about 95% for renewables paired with batteries, declining as more batteries are added. Georgia Power's earlier tiered battery values were 95% for the first 3,000 MW, 75% for the next 3,000 MW and 50% for the next 3,000 MW. In the previous round, for 2029–2031, the utility planned nearly 10,000 MW, about 6,000 MW of it gas.
ProblemAt 20% ELCC, a 1,000 MW battery counts as 200 MW, while a 1,000 MW gas plant at 80% counts as 800 MW. A technical parameter therefore largely decides which technology wins the procurement. Sherrier called it a 'thumb on the scale for gas,' and advocates testified to Georgia regulators. Georgia Power's justification was not available in the retrieved text, and the 20% figure comes from advocates rather than a utility document.
Where & whyThe problem arises in the integrated resource planning and procurement stage. A vertically integrated, rate-regulated utility chooses its own accreditation assumptions, and these are opaque to outsiders. That creates information asymmetry with regulators, and it can align with incentives that favour rate-based gas assets. Uncertainty about large-load growth makes the choice consequential for decades.
Stakeholders
Georgia Power — Must meet peak reliability for new large loads and earns returns on approved capacity.
Georgia Public Service Commission — Approves the selected projects in mid-2027 and must judge whether the accreditation method is sound.
Battery and clean-energy developers (e.g., Advanced Power Alliance members) — A low ELCC makes their bids uncompetitive per accredited MW.
Data centres and other ratepayers — Bear the cost and emissions profile of whichever capacity mix is built.
Southern Environmental Law Center — Seeks a fair valuation of storage and lower fossil lock-in.
Research hints
💡 Use a capacity-accreditation lens. Compare ELCC methods across ISOs and vertically integrated utilities and ask how sensitive procurement outcomes are to them.
💡 Consider a principal–agent setting between a regulator and a utility that controls the technical parameters of its own planning model.
💡 Puzzle: declining marginal ELCC for storage is real, but why the drop from about 75% to 20%? Saturation, peak-shape changes from data-centre load, or strategic choice?
Data leadsGeorgia PSC IRP and procurement dockets; EIA-860 for planned gas and storage units; PJM and other ISO published ELCC class ratings for comparison.
Keywordscapacity markets · battery storage · gas turbines · data center electricity demand · load forecasting
03 · Trade & Industrial Policy

Canada gives Stellantis 30 days to cure default on C$529M EV subsidy after Jeep production moves to Illinois

ET Manufacturing · Nov 14 · Read · ☆ Star

On 12 Nov 2026 Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said Ottawa had sent Stellantis a notice of default under a 2022 agreement. Under it, Canada committed up to C$529M (about US$380M) to retool the Brampton and Windsor plants for EVs. Stellantis has 30 days to cure or face a lawsuit. Jeep Compass production moved to Illinois amid US tariffs, leaving more than 2,000 Canadian workers without jobs. On 9 Nov Stellantis agreed to sell the idled Brampton plant to armoured-vehicle maker Roshel for an undisclosed price.

BackgroundCanada and Ontario have used large subsidies to anchor EV and auto capacity. US tariffs are now raising the cost of serving the US market from Canadian plants. Stellantis offered to repay funding received for Brampton. Stellantis Canada CEO Trevor Longley said it would return C$24M, which Ottawa considers short of what is owed. Joly says the company is 'on the hook for hundreds of millions of dollars.'
ProblemThe dispute tests whether subsidy agreements with performance conditions can hold mobile production capacity when trade policy shifts the cost of locating plants. Canada argues Stellantis breached the deal by abandoning Brampton production without naming a replacement vehicle. Open questions include the size of any clawback, whether a suit will actually be filed, and whether the Roshel sale counts as mitigation. PM Mark Carney called the sale a partial solution.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the plant-allocation decision of a multinational automaker. Tariffs create an incentive to consolidate production inside the US, while subsidy contracts written before the tariffs are incomplete about such shocks. The government's leverage is limited to clawbacks and litigation, which may be small relative to the tariff savings from relocating.
Stakeholders
Stellantis — Wants to minimise tariff exposure and repayment liability while keeping its relationship with Canada.
Government of Canada and Ontario — Want to recover subsidy value, protect jobs and keep the credibility of future incentive deals.
Unifor and Brampton workers — More than 2,000 jobs lost; seek a replacement product or new work.
Roshel — Acquires an idled plant to expand armoured-vehicle production.
Research hints
💡 Use a contract-theory lens: subsidies as relational contracts with clawback clauses, and how renegotiation risk changes once tariffs make relocation attractive.
💡 Run an event study around the tariff announcements, the 9 Nov sale and the 12 Nov default notice. Compare with prior reshoring announcement studies.
💡 Compare plant-closure governance: how firms manage stakeholders when exits are policy-induced rather than demand-driven.
Data leadsBTS TransBorder and USITC DataWeb for Canada–US vehicle trade flows; SEC EDGAR filings for Stellantis disclosures; Statistics Canada vehicle production data; Canadian strategic innovation fund contribution records.
Keywordsreshoring · plant closure · industrial policy · litigation risk · trade diversion
Related research
Shock and Awe: A Theoretical Framework and Data Sources for Studying the Impact of 2025 Tariffs on Global Supply Chains (Journal of Supply Chain Management, 2025)
Lays out a framework and data sources for studying how the 2025 US tariffs reshape supply chains, directly applicable to the Jeep Compass relocation.
People, performance and transition: A case study of psychological contract and stakeholder orientation in the Toyota Australia plant closure (Journal of Operations Management, 2022)
A case study of how Toyota Australia managed workforce and stakeholder obligations through a plant closure, a useful comparison for Brampton.
04 · Critical Minerals & Batteries

France's large EV battery recycling plants stalled on thin feedstock as smaller and mid-sized projects advance

Latin Times · Nov 14 · Read · ☆ Star

A 14 Nov 2026 Latin Times feature revisits the autumn 2024 collapse of France's flagship battery recycling projects. Stellantis–Orano dropped its joint venture in September 2024, and Eramet suspended its 50,000 t/yr ReLieVe plant in Dunkirk (about 200,000 EVs' worth of modules) on 24 Oct 2024. Smaller projects continued. Battri opened a black-mass plant in Pas-de-Calais in June 2025. LG Energy Solution and Derichebourg formed a 50/50 joint venture in April 2025 for a plant handling more than 20,000 t/yr, with operations planned for 2027. Orano is building a Dunkirk cathode and recycling platform targeting operation by end-2026.

BackgroundFrance had about 1.5M EVs in early 2026, too few of them at end of life to feed large plants. Europe also lacks a mature cathode industry to buy recycled metal salts. Eramet cited uncertain raw-material supply because European battery factories had not ramped up. Its pilot plant in Trappes is still running and reports recovery rates above 90% for nickel, cobalt and lithium. The EU Batteries Regulation requires 70% recycling efficiency for lithium batteries by 31 Dec 2030. The plant timelines above come only from this article and were not independently checked.
ProblemRecyclers must commit capacity years before end-of-life feedstock exists, and before downstream buyers of recycled material exist. Large plants failed on both the input and output sides, while smaller black-mass and partnership-based plants proceeded. The open question is what scale and degree of vertical integration make recycling viable during the transition.
Where & whyThe friction sits at the reverse-logistics and processing stage. Feedstock depends on lagged EV fleet turnover and on scrap from gigafactories, and offtake depends on a cathode industry that barely exists in Europe. This creates a two-sided coordination failure under heavy uncertainty, and regulatory targets alone do not resolve it.
Stakeholders
Large incumbents (Eramet, Suez, Orano, Stellantis) — Avoid stranded capital, but risk losing a position in European recycled materials.
Smaller and mid-sized recyclers (Battri, LGES–Derichebourg) — Seek modular, lower-risk entry with secured feedstock or offtake.
EU and French policymakers — Need domestic recycling capacity to meet Batteries Regulation targets and raw-material security goals.
Cathode and cell makers — Potential buyers of recycled nickel, cobalt and lithium salts whose own ramp-up is uncertain.
Research hints
💡 Capacity-sizing and timing models with stochastic, lagged returns (closed-loop supply chain models with fleet-turnover dynamics) fit this case. When does modular, smaller capacity dominate a single large plant?
💡 Compare the cancelled large projects with those that proceeded. Did a gigafactory partner or vertical integration (as with LGES) determine survival?
💡 Ask whether the 2030 recycling-efficiency mandate shapes capacity decisions, or whether feedstock volume does.
Data leadsEurostat waste-battery statistics; ACEA and AVERE EV registration data for modelling end-of-life flows; Benchmark or BNEF battery recycling capacity trackers, with company announcements as a public fallback.
Keywordsbattery recycling · capacity investment under uncertainty · fleet turnover · secondary materials · closed-loop supply chain
Related research
Accelerating vehicle fleet turnover to achieve sustainable mobility goals (Journal of Operations Management, 2022)
Models how slowly the vehicle fleet turns over, which governs when end-of-life EV batteries become available as recycling feedstock.
05 · Circular Economy

Tianjin's 15th Five-Year circular economy plan directs where secondary copper capacity is built and leans on scrap and black-mass imports

Shanghai Metals Market · Nov 15 · Read · ☆ Star

On 5 Nov 2026 the Tianjin Municipal Development and Reform Commission issued its Circular Economy Development 15th Five-Year Plan. Its 2030 targets include about 1.5M t/yr of major renewable resources recycled, recycling-industry output value up about 27% on 2025, 10M t of capacity for processing scrap steel, nonferrous metals, paper, plastics and rubber, 6,000 recycling outlets and 20 sorting centres. For secondary copper, aluminium and lead, it calls for capacity early-warning by category, orderly exit of outdated capacity, and steering new capacity to the Ziya zone and the Tianjin Port Free Trade Zone.

BackgroundChina's secondary-metal sector relies on fragmented collection from individuals and on imported scrap. The plan says to use import policies for secondary copper, aluminium and steel and for recycled lithium-battery black mass. It also seeks national approval for overseas recycled-material distribution and processing sites in Tianjin. On the demand side, it encourages electronics makers to use more recycled copper and aluminium. On tax, it backs 'reverse invoicing', in which recyclers issue invoices when they buy scrap from individuals, while cracking down on invoice fraud. SMM's view that the plan reshapes the industry is its own analysis, not the plan's wording.
ProblemLocal planners are trying to control capacity location and size in a scrap-processing industry prone to overcapacity, while also expanding throughput and imports. Concentrating capacity in designated zones could improve oversight and logistics but could raise costs for dispersed collectors. Reverse-invoicing rules address a tax-compliance gap that has long distorted scrap procurement.
Where & whyThe friction is at the collection and first-processing link. Informal sellers cannot issue invoices, which creates information asymmetry and invites tax fraud. Scrap supply is variable and partly import-dependent. Fragmented, outdated smelting capacity is an environmental and coordination problem that the plan addresses through administrative capacity steering.
Stakeholders
Secondary copper, aluminium and lead smelters in Tianjin — Face relocation into zones, exit of outdated capacity, and new import opportunities.
Scrap collectors and individual sellers — Affected by reverse-invoicing compliance and by the sorting-centre network.
Electronics manufacturers — Encouraged to raise recycled-metal content, which affects input sourcing.
Overseas scrap and black-mass exporters — Could gain a northern Chinese import and processing hub.
Research hints
💡 Treat zone-based capacity steering and outdated-capacity exit as a regional policy shock. Compare Tianjin's scrap import flows with other Chinese ports.
💡 Reverse invoicing is an information-design problem in procurement from informal suppliers. Consider how tax traceability changes recyclers' sourcing costs.
💡 Puzzle: the plan combines capacity early-warning with expansion targets. How do planners avoid recreating overcapacity?
Data leadsUN Comtrade and China customs (GACC) data on copper scrap (HS 7404) and black-mass imports by port; SMM secondary copper price and operating-rate series.
Keywordssecondary materials · industrial policy · facility siting · capacity caps · recycled content
Related research
Creating the competitive edge: A new relationship between operations management and industrial policy (Journal of Operations Management, 2017)
Argues that OM research should engage directly with industrial policy, a framing for studying how local plans direct the location and structure of recycling capacity.
Thread to pull

Three stories turn on how an administrative parameter allocates risk around capacity. Georgia's battery ELCC decides which technology counts as reliable, Ireland's self-supply rule shifts adequacy risk onto data centres, and Canada's clawback terms determine what a firm pays to move a plant. Comparing how such parameters are set, contested in court or before regulators, and anticipated by investors could connect energy-planning and industrial-policy research through a common lens of skin in the game.

New in the journals

The Economics of Waste Valorization
Management Science

The paper studies waste valorization, turning a production byproduct into a distinct saleable product, using a two-stage stochastic production model. It derives optimal production quantities when the main product and the valorized product are linked in both production and demand.

Battery black mass and secondary copper are byproduct-to-product flows of this kind, so the model speaks to the feedstock and offtake interdependence behind France's stalled recyclers and Tianjin's import strategy.

What’s at Stake? Understanding the Role of Home Equity in Flood Insurance Demand
Management Science

Flood insurance take-up rises and falls with homeowners' equity. Using purchase-timing differences during the housing bust, the authors show that less skin in the game causally reduces insurance demand, shifting climate risk to lenders and governments.

The same risk-shifting logic applies to subsidy and connection deals: when firms have little at stake, as with Stellantis's partial repayment offer or data centres' fossil backup, risk moves to the public.
